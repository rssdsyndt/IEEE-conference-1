\begin{abstract}
Explanations are widely assumed to increase user trust in AI-based recommender
systems, yet accumulating evidence indicates that the effect depends on the kind
of explanation provided rather than on its mere presence. Comparative evidence on
explanation types remains scarce in conversational recommender systems, and is
almost absent in sensory-hedonic domains, where the defining product attribute
cannot be transmitted through a screen. This study reports a between-subjects
experiment with 90 participants, 30 per condition, using a conversational
recommender system for perfume in which a large language model handles dialogue
while a knowledge-graph embedding backend produces the recommendations.
Explanation type was manipulated purely through system-prompt conditioning, with
no fine-tuning, across three conditions: feature-based, narrative-based, and
comparative-based. Perceived trust and purchase intention were measured with
adapted two-item scales and analysed using Kruskal-Wallis and Dunn tests.
Explanation type significantly affected both trust and purchase intention, with a
large and a medium-to-large effect size respectively. Contrary to the common
design intuition that warmer and more relatable explanations build trust,
narrative-based explanations produced the lowest trust, significantly below both
objective-grounded conditions. Comparative-based explanations produced the highest
purchase intention, significantly above the other two conditions. The association
between trust and purchase intention was positive but weak (Spearman rho = 0.229,
p = 0.030), and no evidence of moderation by perfume familiarity was found. The
two design goals were therefore not achieved by the same condition, which
indicates a concrete design trade-off for conversational recommender systems in
hedonic domains.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% ===============================================================

Conversational recommender systems (CRS) support a task-oriented, multi-turn dialogue
in which the system elicits a user's current preferences, provides explanations for its
suggestions, and processes feedback \cite{jannach2021}. Large language models (LLMs)
have made such systems inexpensive to build and natural to converse with, which sharpens
a design question that predates them: when a recommender explains itself, what should it
actually say?

Explainable AI (XAI) is widely positioned as the bridge between opaque recommendation
models and users who must decide how far to rely on them; a systematic review of 97 user
studies of model explanations organises the field around trust, understanding, usability,
and human-AI collaboration performance \cite{rong2024}. Within recommender systems
specifically, the canonical framing holds that explanations may serve seven aims ---
effectiveness, satisfaction, transparency, scrutability, trust, persuasiveness, and
efficiency --- and, critically, that these aims can be incompatible, so that an
explanation optimised for one may degrade another \cite{tintarev2012}. Consumer research
supports the optimistic half of this framing: across four studies, the presence of post
hoc explanations raised interpretability perceptions and, through them, trust, purchase
intention, and click-through on AI recommendations \cite{chen2024}.

The pessimistic half receives less attention in practice. In an experiment with 215
participants, feature-importance explanations produced higher trust than counterfactual
explanations, and explanations raised trust even when the underlying system performed
poorly, a pattern the authors read as overreliance rather than warranted trust
\cite{brito2023}. Qualitative work on explanation interfaces reaches a compatible
conclusion, documenting trust calibration errors in which users agree or disagree with a
recommendation without deliberation \cite{naiseh2021}. Together, these results move the
question from whether an explanation is present to which kind of explanation is given.

Two gaps follow, and this paper addresses both.

\textit{First, explanation types are seldom compared against one another inside a single
conversational system.} The foundational demonstration that explanation type determines
which component of trust is affected --- \textit{how} explanations raising competence and
benevolence beliefs, \textit{why} explanations raising benevolence, and
\textit{trade-off} explanations raising integrity --- was obtained on a static
recommendation agent \cite{wang2007}. More recent comparative work contrasts
feature-importance with counterfactual explanations \cite{brito2023}, or organises
contrastive and counterfactual generation methods into a taxonomy \cite{stepin2021}, but
does so outside conversational e-commerce. A head-to-head comparison of feature-based,
narrative-based, and comparative-based justification within one LLM-driven CRS has not,
to the authors' knowledge, been reported.

\textit{Second, sensory-hedonic domains remain underexplored relative to utilitarian
ones}, and the difference is consequential rather than cosmetic. The facilitating effect
of post hoc explanations is stronger in the utilitarian than in the hedonic
decision-making domain, and explanation type interacts with domain: attribute-based
explanations are more effective at enhancing trust in utilitarian settings, whereas
user-based explanations are more effective in hedonic ones \cite{chen2024}. Clickstream
evidence converges from a different angle, showing that negative reviews reduce purchase
probability and increase search for substitutes for utilitarian products but not for
hedonic ones, and for reviews concerning product functionality or customer service but
not for taste-related factors \cite{varga2024}. Product type similarly moderates the path
from perceived deception to online repurchase intention, with hedonic purchases proving
less sensitive \cite{wangx2022}. Findings obtained in rational domains therefore cannot
be assumed to transfer.

Perfume is an extreme case of the hedonic condition. Its defining attribute cannot be
transmitted through a screen at all, unlike the visual attributes of clothing or the
auditory attributes of music. Its descriptive vocabulary, comprising terms such as
accord, sillage, and olfactory family, is commanded mainly by enthusiasts, which makes
domain familiarity a plausible moderator of how a feature-based justification is
received. The resulting purchase uncertainty is correspondingly high. If explanation
design has domain-dependent effects, perfume is where they should be most visible.

Accordingly, this study asks three questions. \textbf{RQ1}: does the type of
justification a conversational recommender offers affect perceived trust and purchase
intention? \textbf{RQ2}: is perceived trust associated with purchase intention?
\textbf{RQ3}: does perfume familiarity moderate the effect of justification type?

The contributions are as follows.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{An artefact.} A perfume CRS in which an LLM conducts the dialogue and a
knowledge graph embedding backend selects the items, and in which three justification
logics are instantiated by system-prompt conditioning alone, without fine-tuning.
Because item selection is delegated to a backend whose scoring procedure is identical in
every condition, the manipulation operates on how the system reasons aloud rather than
on the recommendation procedure itself.

\item \textbf{Comparative evidence from a hedonic domain.} A between-subjects field
experiment with 90 participants, conducted in a physical perfume store where the
recommended fragrances could actually be smelled, establishes that justification type
significantly affects both perceived trust and purchase intention. This responds directly
to the call for domain-sensitive evidence raised in \cite{chen2024}.

\item \textbf{A counter-intuitive result with theoretical grounding.} Narrative-based
justification, designed to be the warmest and most relatable of the three, produced the
\textit{lowest} trust. The result inverts a prevailing design heuristic and is consistent
with evidence that a competent conversational style outperforms a warm one on
cognition-oriented tasks, mediated by agent-mind perception, while the reverse holds for
emotion-oriented tasks \cite{libaoku2024}.

\item \textbf{An explicit design trade-off.} The condition that maximised trust and the
condition that maximised purchase intention were not the same, and the association
between the two outcomes proved weak. This supplies empirical support for the claim that
explanatory aims can conflict \cite{tintarev2012}, and indicates that comparative
justification reaches purchase intention by a route that does not pass through trust.

\item \textbf{Transparent treatment of null and weak results.} No evidence of moderation
by familiarity was found, and the constraints that qualify this conclusion, together with
those affecting a supplementary behavioural measure, are reported rather than suppressed.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews
explanation types in XAI and recommender systems, explanations in CRS, trust and purchase
intention, hedonic product domains, and familiarity as a boundary condition.
Section~\ref{sec:system} describes the system and the three conditions.
Section~\ref{sec:method} presents the method. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the
results, Section~\ref{sec:discussion} interprets them and states the limitations, and
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% ===============================================================

\subsection{Explanation Types in XAI and Recommender Systems}

Tintarev and Masthoff established the reference framing for explanation in recommender
systems, in which explanations may serve seven aims and any evaluation must state which
aim it targets because the aims can be incompatible \cite{tintarev2012}. Their own
empirical studies illustrate the tension concretely: across two domains, personalising
feature-based explanations proved detrimental to effectiveness even though it may improve
user satisfaction, a result the authors report as contrary to their hypothesis. The
structural lesson --- that a more appealing explanation can be a less useful one --- bears
directly on the present study.

Wang and Benbasat supplied the foundational demonstration that it is explanation
\textit{type}, not explanation presence, that determines which component of trust is
affected \cite{wang2007}. In a laboratory experiment with a purpose-built recommendation
agent, the use of \textit{how} explanations increased competence and benevolence beliefs,
\textit{why} explanations increased benevolence beliefs, and \textit{trade-off}
explanations increased integrity beliefs. Their conclusion, that the effects of
explanations depend largely on the contents and types of explanations provided, is the
premise on which this study rests.

Subsequent work has sharpened the picture without settling it. Stepin et al. conducted a
systematic review of contrastive and counterfactual explanation and proposed a taxonomy
covering both theoretical accounts and computational frameworks \cite{stepin2021}. De
Brito Duarte et al. compared feature-importance against counterfactual explanations with
215 participants and found trust higher under feature importance, while also observing
that explanations increased trust even when the system was unreliable; they further note
that other work suggests counterfactual explanations may be particularly advantageous
where expert knowledge is available \cite{brito2023}. Naiseh et al. approach the same
tension from the design side, deriving five principles intended to support calibrated
rather than maximal trust \cite{naiseh2021}.

Two broader observations frame these results. Reviewing 97 user studies of model
explanations, Rong et al. concluded that the effectiveness of XAI in users' interaction
with models is not consistent across applications, and that such evaluations rarely draw
on cognitive or social science \cite{rong2024}. Domain-specific studies accordingly
measure perception directly; de Campos et al., for instance, evaluate an explainable
content-based recommender against transparency, satisfaction, trust, and scrutability in
a user study \cite{decampos2024}.

The anchor for the present work is Chen et al., who examined consumer responses to
explainable AI recommendations across four studies \cite{chen2024}. The presence of post
hoc explanations increased interpretability perceptions and, through them, trust,
purchase intention, and click-through; the facilitating effect was stronger in the
utilitarian than in the hedonic decision-making domain; and explanation type modulated
effectiveness, with attribute-based explanations more effective at enhancing trust in
utilitarian domains and user-based explanations more effective in hedonic ones. Their own
conclusion is that there are no universally optimal post hoc explanations. The present
study carries this line from static recommendation displays into multi-turn conversation
and, as Section~\ref{sec:results} reports, obtains a result that runs against the
direction their third finding would predict.

\subsection{Explanations in Conversational Recommender Systems}

Jannach et al., surveying the CRS field, observe that although explanations are often
considered a main feature of a convincing dialogue, these aspects are not explored a great
deal \cite{jannach2021}. The intervening years have narrowed the gap only partially.

Hernandez-Bocanegra and Ziegler investigated conversational explanation delivery directly,
contrasting a GUI-based dialogue with a chatbot-like natural-language interface while
varying the degree of interactivity, and relating perceived explanation quality to
transparency, trust, and effectiveness through structural equation modelling
\cite{ziegler2023}. Providing interactive options for scrutinising explanatory arguments
had a significant positive influence on user evaluations. Their manipulation, however,
concerns interface and interactivity rather than the content of the explanation.

Siro et al. annotated CRS dialogues along six aspects --- relevance, interestingness,
understanding, task completion, interest arousal, and efficiency --- at both turn and
dialogue level, and found that the concept of satisfaction varies per user, with
relevance the significant factor at turn level \cite{siro2023}. Rana et al. studied
critiquing-based conversational recommendation with 228 participants and showed that the
choice of underlying recommendation algorithm materially affects critiquing behaviour,
failure rates, dialogue length, and satisfaction \cite{rana2024}.

Methodologically the closest parallel is Ziegfeld et al., who built three versions of a
CRS and ran a between-subjects experiment with 66 participants across ten user experience
constructs \cite{ziegfeld2025}. They found no significant effects of the three preference
elicitation methods on any questionnaire-based construct, although objective measures such
as chat duration and recommendation match score did differ. Their manipulation targeted
preference elicitation rather than justification content; the contrast with the present
findings is taken up in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\subsection{Trust and Purchase Intention in Recommender Systems}

Trust in a technological artefact is conventionally decomposed into beliefs about
competence, benevolence, and integrity \cite{mcknight2002}, and this structure underlies
the explanation-to-trust mapping reported in \cite{wang2007}. For user-centric evaluation
of recommenders, the ResQue framework supplies validated constructs together with design
guidance, including the guidance that explaining \textit{why} the system recommends an
item increases transparency whereas explaining \textit{trade-offs} increases persuasion
\cite{pu2012} --- a distinction that maps closely onto the comparison drawn in this study.
Perceived usefulness is taken in the technology acceptance sense, as the degree to which a
person believes that using a system would enhance performance \cite{davis1989}.

The presumed chain from trust to purchase behaviour has been validated in the chatbot
context by Yen and Chiang, who combined 204 questionnaire responses with EEG evidence and
found credibility, competence, anthropomorphism, social presence, and informativeness to
influence trust in chatbots, which in turn affected purchase intention \cite{yen2021}.
Notably, neither media richness nor playfulness positively influenced trust, which the
authors attribute to communication with a shopping chatbot being a functional task. This
is an early signal of the pattern the present study observes.

Work on anthropomorphism qualifies the chain further. Cheng et al. found perceived warmth
and perceived competence to positively affect trust in chatbots while communication delay
reduced it, and, critically, that exchange relationships strengthen the importance of
perceived competence on trust whereas communal relationships do not moderate the effect
of warmth \cite{cheng2022}. Li et al. modelled parallel pathways in which chatbot warmth
and competence affect both trust and chatbot overload, with both in turn shaping service
evaluation and then customer purchase, implying that trust is not the sole channel to
behavioural outcomes \cite{liyang2025}. Crolic et al. provide the strongest cautionary
precedent: across five studies, when customers enter a chatbot-led interaction in an angry
emotional state, chatbot anthropomorphism has a negative effect on satisfaction, firm
evaluation, and subsequent purchase intentions, driven by expectancy violations caused by
inflated pre-encounter expectations of chatbot efficacy \cite{crolic2022}. The premise
that a more human-seeming agent is a better agent is therefore already contested.

Most directly relevant, Li et al. examined anthropomorphic interaction style through the
lens of mind perception across three experiments \cite{libaoku2024}. For cognition-oriented
service tasks, a competent interaction style improved consumer responses more effectively
than a warm one, with agent-mind perception mediating; for emotion-oriented tasks a warm
style improved responses, mediated by experience-mind perception. Their claim that no
style is universally superior, and that task type sets the boundary, supplies the
mechanism this paper invokes in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\subsection{Sensory and Hedonic Product Domains}

Hedonic products are judged against criteria that are subjective and unstable relative to
the practical and comparatively stable criteria applied to utilitarian goods. Wang et al.
showed across three studies that perceived deception damages online repurchase intention
less for hedonic than for utilitarian products, attributing the difference to hedonic
evaluation being more fluctuant and susceptible to change \cite{wangx2022}. Using
clickstream data from a major online retailer, Varga and Albuquerque found that negative
reviews significantly reduce purchase probability and increase search for substitutes, but
that these effects apply to utilitarian products and not to hedonic ones, and to reviews
concerning product functionality or customer service but not to taste-related factors
\cite{varga2024}. Chen et al., as noted, found the effect of explanation itself to be
domain-contingent \cite{chen2024}.

The cumulative implication is that XAI evidence gathered in rational domains cannot be
assumed to transfer to sensory ones. To the authors' knowledge, no comparative test of
XAI explanation types has been reported in the olfactory domain.

\subsection{Familiarity as a Boundary Condition}

Domain familiarity is a plausible moderator of explanation effectiveness. Using an
AI-based decision support system in the chess domain with 100 participants, Bayer et al.
found that domain-specific expertise negatively affects trust, and, more pointedly for the
present design, that users with high domain-specific knowledge are more willing to trust
the system when an explanation is provided whereas users with low domain-specific
knowledge are not \cite{bayer2023}. Their conclusion is that explanations must fit the
user.

Celar and Byrne provide the closest design analogue, crossing explanation type with domain
familiarity across four experiments with 731 participants \cite{celar2023}. Participants
judged counterfactual explanations more helpful than causal ones; counterfactuals did not
improve the accuracy of their predictions of the AI's decisions, but did improve the
accuracy of their own decisions; and the effect of familiarity depended on whether the AI
was correct. Their sample size is a useful reference point when interpreting null
moderation results obtained on smaller samples.

\subsection{Research Positioning}

Three antecedents locate this study most precisely. Wang and Benbasat \cite{wang2007}
compared explanation types and decomposed trust into three dimensions, but did so on a
static recommendation agent in a utilitarian domain. Chen et al. \cite{chen2024} compared
domains and crossed presence with type, but on static recommendation displays rather than
in dialogue. Ziegfeld et al. \cite{ziegfeld2025} ran a between-subjects experiment inside a
multi-turn CRS, but manipulated preference elicitation rather than justification, and
found no effect on subjective measures.

The present study combines the three properties that these antecedents hold separately:
justification types are compared against one another rather than against a
no-explanation baseline; the comparison takes place inside a multi-turn, LLM-driven
conversation; and the domain is sensory-hedonic, with a core attribute that cannot be
conveyed through the interface at all. In addition, the narrative condition operationalises
affective explanation \textit{content}, which is distinct from the conversational
\textit{style} manipulations that dominate the anthropomorphism literature
\cite{libaoku2024}, \cite{cheng2022}.

\subsection{Architecture Overview}

The experimental platform is Aromatique AI, a conversational recommender agent for
perfume, deployed as a web application and operated on site in a physical perfume store.
Its architecture separates two responsibilities that LLM-based recommenders frequently
fuse: \textit{language} and \textit{recommendation}. GPT-4o-mini conducts the dialogue,
extracts preferences, and verbalises the justification; a conversational-recommendation
hybrid knowledge graph embedding (CR-HKGE) backend selects the items. Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}
shows the arrangement.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig_architecture}}
\caption{System architecture. The LLM conducts the dialogue and phrases the
justification; item selection is delegated to the CR-HKGE backend, whose scoring
procedure is identical in every condition. The condition is set once, at the
system-prompt layer, at the start of each session, and governs both how preferences are
elicited and how the recommendation is justified.}
\label{fig:arch}
\end{figure}

Two properties of this arrangement bear on the interpretation of the experiment.

First, \textit{the backend is a knowledge graph embedding model rather than a
retrieval-augmented generation pipeline}. Recommendations are produced by scoring
candidate items in a learned embedding space over a structured perfume knowledge graph,
not by retrieving and summarising documents. The graph holds 998 entities and 9,255
triples across seven relation types: 340 catalogue products, 312 attribute entities on the
catalogue side (17 olfactory families, 110 accords, 185 visual notes), and a
cross-reference layer of 346 entities comprising 241 externally recognised perfumes with
their 60 accords and 45 families, which supports inspired-by and semantic-similarity
links. Selecting three items from 340 is therefore a non-trivial retrieval problem rather
than a choice among a handful of alternatives. The design follows the family of
knowledge-graph recommenders that aggregate collaborative and semantic signals through
graph attention \cite{litois2023}, \cite{elahi2024}, including work extending such graphs
to conversational settings in order to capture a user's implicit states, such as current
situation and emotional state, from the dialogue context \cite{yang2025}.

Second, \textit{the LLM acts as an interface rather than as the recommender}. This mirrors
the agent pattern that employs LLMs as the brain and recommender models as tools
\cite{huang2023}, an arrangement motivated by the observation that LLMs lack knowledge of
domain-specific item catalogues, which in e-commerce leads them to propose products that
do not exist. Item selection here is governed by a single scoring procedure identical in
all three conditions; the LLM cannot substitute, reorder, or invent products.

It is worth being precise about what this does and does not guarantee. The recommendation
\textit{algorithm} is invariant across conditions. The recommended \textit{items} are not
guaranteed to be, because the backend is queried with a preference representation that the
dialogue itself produces, and the dialogue differs between conditions in the manner
described next. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} treats this qualification explicitly rather
than assuming it away.

\subsection{Experimental Conditions}

The condition is fixed by system-prompt conditioning at the start of each session, with no
fine-tuning. This follows the prompting paradigm, one of the three paradigms alongside
pre-training and fine-tuning by which LLMs are adapted to recommendation tasks
\cite{zhao2024}, and it makes the independent variable inexpensive to manipulate and
straightforward to replicate.
\PH{Confirm that the venue accepts supplementary material; if so, state here that the
three system prompts are provided as such.}

The manipulated variable is the \textit{justification logic} the agent applies, and it
governs the whole interaction rather than only the moment of recommendation. Each prompt
instructs the agent to hold its assigned logic ``from your first greeting to your final
closing message,'' so the ten elicitation questions are framed differently in each
condition, as are the acknowledgements and the final explanation. Table~\ref{tab:conditions}
gives the parallel form of one elicitation question alongside the corresponding
explanation content.

This is a deliberate design decision rather than an oversight. A system whose
conversational framing contradicted its own explanations would be internally incoherent
and unlikely to occur in deployment. Its cost is that the study manipulates justification
logic as a whole and cannot separate the contribution of explanation content from that of
conversational framing, a limitation stated in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

The contrast is visible in how the three logics render the same product. For one perfume
recommended in all three conditions, condition A produced ``\textit{it belongs to the
AQUATIC family with green and fresh-spicy as its main character --- exactly what you are
looking for in an office setting}''; condition B produced ``\textit{picture a fresh scent
flowing gently, like morning dew, giving you new energy and confidence}''; and condition C
produced ``\textit{it is the freshest and brightest of the three --- it foregrounds
freshness with a woody touch}''. The excerpts come from three different sessions, so the
elicited preferences differ, which is why A frames the product for office use and C for a
relaxed setting. Inspection of the condition C transcripts confirms that comparative
explanations consistently name one or two co-recommended products as explicit comparators.

Conditions A and C have established counterparts in the XAI literature. Condition A
corresponds to attribute-based or feature-importance explanation, the form shown to yield
higher trust than counterfactual explanation among non-expert users \cite{brito2023}.
Condition C corresponds to contrastive explanation, for which a taxonomy of generation
methods exists \cite{stepin2021} and which has been shown to improve the accuracy of
users' own decisions relative to causal explanation \cite{celar2023}.

Condition B is the novel element. The CRS and anthropomorphism literatures examine
conversational \textit{style} on the warmth-competence axis \cite{libaoku2024},
\cite{cheng2022} while treating explanation \textit{content} as fixed. Condition B instead
makes affective content the substance of the justification: it supplies no compositional
facts and no comparative frame, only resonance. It is closest in spirit to the user-based
explanations found more effective in hedonic domains \cite{chen2024}, while differing in
one respect examined in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}: user-based explanations carry social
proof, whereas purely narrative explanations carry none.

One asymmetry between the prompts is recorded here because it bears on a supplementary
analysis reported later. The closing messages in conditions A and C instruct the agent to
end by inviting the user to smell the fragrances at the store's tester area; the closing
message in condition B contains no such instruction.

\subsection{Conversational Interface}

A session proceeds in four stages: greeting and consent; preference elicitation;
presentation of three ranked perfumes with the condition-appropriate explanation; and the
post-session questionnaire.

Elicitation follows a fixed protocol of exactly ten questions, asked one per message, with
the agent instructed never to bundle questions and to keep each reply to two or three
sentences. In the terms introduced by Ziegfeld et al. \cite{ziegfeld2025}, this places the
interface at the \textit{high-guidance, low-restriction} point of the elicitation space:
the system fixes the sequence of topics while responses are given in free-form natural
language. The sole exception is the first question, which records perfume familiarity
through three selectable tags corresponding to beginner, enthusiast, and collector.

The dialogue yields a structured preference record comprising familiarity, occasion,
primary and secondary scent character, intensity, attributes to avoid, and free-text
context. The fields are identical across conditions, but their content is expressed in
condition-specific vocabulary: scent character is captured as attributes in condition A, as
moods in condition B, and as positions on contrasting spectra in condition C. The record is
passed to the CR-HKGE backend, which returns three ranked products with their olfactory
family and main accords; the LLM then phrases the justification in its assigned logic.

Sessions comprised a mean of 20.29 messages exchanged between participant and agent
($SD = 4.67$, range 9 to 55). Length was comparable across conditions --- 20.60 in A, 19.20
in B, and 21.07 in C, a spread of under two messages --- which makes interaction length an
unlikely alternative explanation for the differences reported in
Section~\ref{sec:results}. Variability was greater in condition A ($SD = 7.03$) than in B
($SD = 2.89$) or C ($SD = 2.65$), indicating a small number of unusually long feature-based
sessions rather than a systematic difference in dialogue length.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}
% ===============================================================

\subsection{Experimental Design}

The study uses a single-factor between-subjects design with three levels of justification
logic (A: feature-based; B: narrative-based; C: comparative-based). Assignment was
determined by a session counter, yielding 30 participants per condition.

A between-subjects design was preferred to a within-subjects alternative deliberately. Had
a participant experienced all three logics, the comparison would have become explicit,
inviting demand characteristics and carryover in how the later conditions were judged. The
cost is reduced statistical power, particularly for the interaction test, and this cost is
revisited in the limitations.

\subsection{Participants}

Ninety participants completed the study, 30 in each condition. Perfume familiarity was
recorded at the first elicitation question using three selectable categories, and
recruitment was balanced by quota across them: 30 beginners, 30 enthusiasts, and 30
collectors.

For analysis the three levels were dichotomised, with beginners coded \textit{novice}
($n = 30$) and enthusiasts and collectors together coded \textit{enthusiast} ($n = 60$).
The dichotomisation rests on construct grounds rather than convenience. What separates the
groups for present purposes is not intensity of interest but command of olfactory
vocabulary: enthusiasts and collectors alike understand terms such as accord, sillage, and
top, heart, and base notes, whereas beginners generally do not. Because condition A rests
entirely on that vocabulary, the boundary that matters falls between beginners and
everyone else.

Two consequences follow, both relevant to the moderation test. First, the two-to-one
imbalance between enthusiast and novice is a downstream consequence of this coding
decision, not a failure of recruitment; the sample itself was balanced by design. Second,
the resulting novice cells are small, with 10, 12, and 8 novices in conditions A, B, and C
respectively, against 20, 18, and 22 enthusiasts.

Familiarity was nonetheless evenly distributed across conditions,
$\chi^{2}(2) = 1.200$, $p = .549$, Cram\'{e}r's $V = 0.116$, with all expected counts at or
above 10. Randomisation therefore succeeded on this dimension, which supports the
interpretation that differences between conditions are not attributable to imbalance in
sample composition.

Demographic characteristics beyond familiarity, specifically age and gender, were not
recorded; the consequences are stated in the limitations. Participation was voluntary and
preceded by an on-screen consent notice stating that the system formed part of
collaborative academic research at the authors' institution, that all responses would be
fully anonymised, that the data would be used for academic purposes only, and that
participation could be declined. Participants proceeded only after explicit affirmation.
No personally identifying information was collected, responses were anonymised at the
point of capture, and no compensation was offered that might have induced participation.
The study involved no deception beyond withholding the research hypothesis, no sensitive
personal data, and no vulnerable population. Formal institutional ethics review was not
sought prior to data collection.

were adapted from the ResQue framework for user-centric evaluation of recommender systems
\cite{pu2012}, and usefulness items from the technology acceptance tradition
\cite{davis1989}. All items were administered in Indonesian.

A ninth, binary item labelled \texttt{scent\_trial} recorded whether the participant had

it. In the present setting the distinction is meaningful, because the study took place in
a store where the fragrances were physically available. The item therefore indicates
whether a participant held information the interface itself could not supply, and it is
treated as exploratory throughout.

\subsection{Procedure}

The study was conducted offline, on the premises of a physical perfume store, with the
system running as a web application on site during the store's normal operating sessions.
Each participant read the consent notice, affirmed participation, and then worked through
the ten-question elicitation sequence until three ranked recommendations and their
explanations were delivered; the questionnaire followed immediately. The consent screen
advertised a duration of approximately ten minutes. Because the store's tester area
remained accessible throughout, participants were able to smell the recommended fragrances
physically, and the \texttt{scent\_trial} item records whether they had done so.

Participation arose from an institutional collaboration with the store rather than from a
recruitment campaign, and no compensation was offered. Participants were not told that
justification logic was under investigation, nor that other versions of the system
existed; the prompts explicitly forbid the agent from disclosing which style it is using.

\subsection{Manipulation Check}

To verify that the three conditions were distinguishable from the transcripts alone, a
subsample of 30 session transcripts, 10 per condition, was coded by three independent
sources: the ground-truth condition recorded by the system (\textit{actual}), a coding by
the researcher (\textit{self}), and a coding by an independent rater blind to condition
assignment (\textit{rater}). Agreement was quantified with Cohen's kappa and interpreted
against the benchmarks of Landis and Koch, who assign values of 0.61--0.80 to substantial
and 0.81--1.00 to almost perfect agreement while noting that such divisions are
arbitrary \cite{landis1977}.

The researcher's dual role as experimenter and coder is acknowledged explicitly. Because
self-coding cannot be considered blind, the evidentiary weight of the manipulation check
rests on the independent rater, and the rater-based and self-based coefficients are
reported separately in Section~\ref{sec:results} rather than averaged into a single
figure.

\subsection{Analysis Plan}

\subsubsection{Scale reliability}
Cronbach's alpha is downward-biased and unstable for two-item scales. Reliability was
therefore assessed with the Spearman-Brown coefficient, the estimator examined for
two-item scales in \cite{eisinga2013}. Average inter-item correlations are reported
alongside the coefficients, since with two items the coefficient is a direct function of a
single correlation and that correlation is the more interpretable quantity.
\PH{The Eisinga et al. paper was not available as a source PDF; confirm that its stated
recommendation is Spearman-Brown before submission.}

\subsubsection{Choice of non-parametric tests}
Shapiro-Wilk tests indicated departures from normality in several condition cells, and
Levene's test indicated heterogeneity of variance for perceived trust. Rank-based
procedures were therefore adopted: Kruskal-Wallis for comparisons across the three
conditions, Dunn's test with Holm correction for pairwise comparisons, Spearman's rho for
association, and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for comparisons against a fixed reference
value. Parametric analyses of variance were also computed and are reported for reference,
but inference rests on the rank-based results.

\subsubsection{Effect size}
For Kruskal-Wallis, epsilon-squared was computed as $\varepsilon^{2} = H/(n-1)$, the
rank-based analogue of eta-squared, and is reported for every omnibus test: a p-value
indicates only that an effect exists, whereas $\varepsilon^{2}$ indicates its magnitude.
Rank-biserial correlations are reported for pairwise comparisons.

\subsubsection{Moderation}
The moderation hypothesis was tested with a two-way analysis of variance crossing condition
with dichotomised familiarity. This introduces an acknowledged inconsistency: the main
analyses are rank-based because normality was violated, whereas the moderation test is
parametric. The reason is practical rather than principled, in that no comparably
established rank-based test exists for a two-factor interaction. The inconsistency is
flagged here rather than left for the reader to find, and the result is interpreted with
corresponding caution.

All analyses were conducted in JASP v0.97.1.

\subsection{Manipulation Check}

Agreement on the 30 blind-coded transcripts was high. The independent rater reproduced the
system-recorded condition with $\kappa = 0.900$ ($SE = 0.068$, 95\% CI [0.767, 1.000]), and
agreed with the researcher's coding at the same level, $\kappa = 0.900$. The researcher's
own coding matched the ground truth exactly, $\kappa = 1.000$; because that coding was not
blind, the evidentiary weight rests on the rater-based coefficient, which falls in the
range Landis and Koch label almost perfect \cite{landis1977}.

The two disagreements are informative and both occurred in condition B, where the
independent rater classified narrative transcripts as feature-based. The manipulation was
therefore not perfectly pure, and its impurity ran towards condition A. Since the
hypothesis of interest concerns a difference between B and the objective-grounded
conditions, this direction of error works against the effect reported below, making that
effect a conservative estimate.

\subsection{Instrument Reliability}

Because all four constructs use two-item scales, reliability was estimated with the
Spearman-Brown coefficient. Purchase intention reached 0.801 (95\% CI [0.551, 1.050],
average inter-item $r = 0.670$) and perceived usefulness 0.731 ([0.449, 1.013],
$r = 0.593$). Satisfaction reached 0.689 ([0.413, 0.966], $r = 0.532$), marginally below the
conventional 0.70 threshold, and perceived trust 0.624 ([0.306, 0.941], $r = 0.454$), clearly
below it.

The trust figure is reported without adjustment, but two features qualify how it should be
read. Its confidence interval is wide, spanning 0.306 to 0.941, which reflects the low
precision obtainable from two items and 90 observations rather than a demonstrably poor
scale. Its average inter-item correlation of 0.454 sits within the range normally
considered healthy for a narrow construct, indicating that the two items cohere and that
the limitation is scale length rather than item content. The implications are taken up in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\subsubsection{H1: Justification logic and perceived trust}
A Kruskal-Wallis test found a significant effect of condition on perceived trust,
$H(2) = 16.57$, $p < .001$, $\varepsilon^{2} = 0.186$, a large effect. Dunn's test with Holm
correction located the difference: condition B scored below condition A
($z = 2.713$, $p = .013$, $r_{rb} = -0.397$) and below condition C
($z = -3.985$, $p < .001$, $r_{rb} = 0.564$), while A and C did not differ
($z = -1.272$, $p = .203$). H1 is supported, but the direction is the opposite of the
design intuition that motivated condition B: the warmest, most personal justification
produced the \textit{lowest} trust of the three.

\subsubsection{H2: Justification logic and purchase intention}
Condition also affected purchase intention, $H(2) = 11.88$, $p = .003$,
$\varepsilon^{2} = 0.133$, a medium-to-large effect. Here the pattern differs. Condition C
scored above condition A ($z = -2.326$, $p = .040$, $r_{rb} = 0.343$) and above condition B
($z = -3.365$, $p = .002$, $r_{rb} = 0.492$), whereas A and B did not differ
($z = 1.039$, $p = .299$). H2 is supported, with a single clear winner.

The two patterns do not coincide. Trust was jointly highest in A and C, whereas purchase
intention was highest in C alone.

\subsubsection{H3: Trust and purchase intention}
Perceived trust and purchase intention were positively but weakly associated,
$\rho = 0.229$, $p = .030$, 95\% CI [0.025, 0.437] from 1{,}000 bootstrap replicates. The
parametric counterpart did not reach significance, $r = 0.161$, $p = .129$. H3 is supported
in the rank-based test only, and the association is weak: trust accounts for roughly five
per cent of the variance in purchase intention, and the lower bound of the interval lies
close to zero.

\subsubsection{H4: Familiarity as moderator}
A two-way analysis of variance crossing condition with dichotomised familiarity produced no
evidence of interaction, $F(2, 84) = 0.452$, $p = .638$. The main effect of condition
persisted, $F(2, 84) = 10.57$, $p < .001$, while familiarity had no main effect,
$F(1, 84) = 0.039$, $p = .845$. No evidence of moderation was found. Given novice cell
sizes of 10, 12, and 8 and a Levene test that was itself significant for this design,
$F(5, 84) = 3.153$, $p = .012$, this result is better read as a limitation of power than as
evidence of absence.

\subsection{Supplementary Analyses}

Two further analyses were run, both exploratory.

First, perceived usefulness and satisfaction were tested against the scale midpoint plus
one half-point, 3.5, using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Both exceeded it decisively:
usefulness $V = 3909$, $p < .001$, rank-biserial $= 0.952$; satisfaction $V = 3533$,

as a finding. Participants judged the system useful and satisfying in every condition, so
the trust differences reported above cannot be attributed to one condition being served by
a system that simply worked less well.

Second, 77 of the 90 participants (85.6\%) had already smelled the recommended product at
the time of the questionnaire, a proportion that did not differ significantly across
conditions, $\chi^{2}(2) = 5.035$, $p = .081$, although it was highest in condition C
(96.7\%) and lowest in condition B (76.7\%). Sensory access was therefore near-universal,
which is a consequence of running the study in a store with an open tester area.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ===============================================================

\subsection{Why Narrative Justification Lowered Trust (RQ1)}

Justification logic affected perceived trust with a large effect, and the direction was
the reverse of the intuition that motivated condition B. The warmest and most personal
justification was trusted least. The finding is stated here without hedging because it is
the study's principal contribution, not an anomaly to be explained away.

The most economical account is that emotional narrative weakens the signal of competence.
Li et al. show that no anthropomorphic style is universally superior: a competent
interaction style produces more positive consumer responses on cognition-oriented service
tasks, mediated by agent-mind perception, whereas a warm style prevails on
emotion-oriented tasks, mediated by experience-mind perception \cite{libaoku2024}. The
decisive variable is the nature of the \textit{task}, not the nature of the
\textit{product}. Perfume is hedonic as a product, but selecting one through a
recommender is an evaluative task: the user is weighing options, assessing fit, and
deciding. On that reading the observed ordering is exactly what the mind-perception
account predicts.

Two further results converge. Cheng et al. find that exchange relationships strengthen the
importance of perceived competence on trust, while communal relationships do not amplify
the effect of warmth \cite{cheng2022}; an in-store purchase consultation is an exchange
relationship in the plainest sense. Yen and Chiang, examining what predicts trust in
shopping chatbots, report that neither media richness nor playfulness had a positive
influence, reasoning that communication with such a chatbot is a functional task
\cite{yen2021}. The present result extends that logic from conversational decoration to
the substance of the justification itself.

That anthropomorphic warmth can be counterproductive is also established. Crolic et al.
trace a negative effect of chatbot anthropomorphism to expectancy violations caused by
inflated pre-encounter expectations of efficacy \cite{crolic2022}. The mechanism differs
from ours, but the moral is shared: humanlike framing raises expectations that the system
must then meet.

The finding stands in apparent tension with the anchor study. Chen et al. report that
attribute-based explanations are more effective at enhancing trust in utilitarian domains,
whereas user-based explanations are more effective in hedonic ones \cite{chen2024}. Two
differences reconcile the accounts. First, their explanations accompany a static
recommendation display, whereas ours are sustained across a ten-question dialogue. In
conversation the user accumulates evidence about the system's competence, not only about
the product, and a narrative that reads as evocative in a single display can read as
evasive when maintained over twenty messages. Second, user-based and narrative-based
explanations are not the same object. A user-based explanation carries social proof; a
purely narrative one carries no evidence at all, only resonance. Our condition B isolates
affect from evidence in a way that user-based explanation does not.

Seen this way the result is less exotic than it first appears. Tintarev and Masthoff
reported that personalising feature-based explanations was detrimental to effectiveness
even as it plausibly improved satisfaction, contrary to their own hypothesis
\cite{tintarev2012}. A more appealing explanation that is a less useful one is a
documented pattern, not a novelty.

\subsection{Why the Two Objective-Grounded Conditions Converged}

Feature-based and comparative-based justification did not differ on trust
($p = .203$). Both anchor the recommendation in something checkable: A in the
composition of the fragrance, C in its position relative to named alternatives. Either
way the system displays command of the domain, which is what an evaluative task rewards.
This is consistent with de Brito Duarte et al., who found feature-importance explanations
to yield higher trust than counterfactual ones among non-expert users \cite{brito2023},
and with the broader premise that explanation type determines which component of trust is
engaged \cite{wang2007}.

\subsection{A Thin Bridge from Trust to Purchase Intention (RQ2)}

Trust and purchase intention were positively but weakly associated, and the parametric
coefficient did not reach significance. The design does not license a causal claim, and
the correct summary is that a weak positive association was observed.

The more informative evidence is structural. Trust was jointly highest in conditions A and
C, whereas purchase intention was highest in C alone. Were trust the principal route to
purchase intention, condition A should have followed condition C upward. It did not. The
implication is that comparative justification acts on purchase intention through a channel
that does not pass through trust, most plausibly by reducing decision uncertainty at the
point of choice. Celar and Byrne provide direct support: counterfactual explanations
improved the accuracy of participants' \textit{own} decisions more than causal ones did
\cite{celar2023}. Contrastive information helps people decide for themselves, which is
precisely what is needed when the decisive attribute cannot be conveyed by the interface.

This is consistent with models in which trust is one channel among several. Li et al.
model warmth and competence as feeding both trust and chatbot overload, with both
pathways shaping service evaluation and subsequent purchase \cite{liyang2025}. It also
qualifies the trust-centric reading of the chatbot literature, in which the trust to
purchase intention chain has been validated as the primary mechanism \cite{yen2021}. A
further caution applies in the other direction: maximal trust is not the design goal, and
explanation interfaces can produce agreement that has not been deliberated
\cite{naiseh2021}.

\subsection{No Evidence of Moderation by Familiarity (RQ3)}

The interaction between condition and familiarity was far from significant. This is
reported as an absence of evidence rather than evidence of absence, and the distinction
matters here because the theoretical grounds for expecting moderation were strong. Bayer
et al. found that domain expertise reduces trust in explainable decision support, and,
more pointedly, that the effect of explanation on trusting intention is itself moderated
by expertise, with high-knowledge users trusting more when an explanation is provided and
low-knowledge users not \cite{bayer2023}.

Two features of the present design limit what can be concluded. The novice cells contained
10, 12, and 8 participants, which is too few to detect an interaction of plausible size;
by comparison, Celar and Byrne required four experiments and 731 participants to
characterise how explanation type interacts with domain familiarity \cite{celar2023}.
Dichotomising three familiarity levels into two may also have blunted a gradient, since
enthusiasts and collectors were merged despite differing in depth of experience.

\subsection{Theoretical Implications}

Three implications follow.

First, and most substantively, the classification that governs explanation design may be
the type of \textit{task} rather than the type of \textit{product}. The XAI and marketing
literatures typically partition domains into utilitarian and hedonic \cite{chen2024},
\cite{wangx2022}, \cite{varga2024}. Our result suggests a cross-cutting axis: whether the
user's immediate activity is evaluative or experiential. Perfume is hedonic on the first
axis and evaluative on the second, and it was the second that predicted the outcome
\cite{libaoku2024}. This reframing is testable, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} proposes
how.

Second, the results provide empirical support for the claim that explanatory aims can be
incompatible \cite{tintarev2012}. Here the incompatibility is concrete rather than
theoretical: the condition maximising trust and the condition maximising purchase
intention were not the same, and the association between the two outcomes was weak.

Third, the study speaks to the sensitivity of subjective measures in CRS research.
Ziegfeld et al., using a comparable three-condition between-subjects design, found no
significant effects on any of ten questionnaire-based user experience constructs, while
objective measures did differ \cite{ziegfeld2025}. Their manipulation concerned preference
elicitation method; ours concerned justification logic. The contrast suggests, without
establishing, that how a system justifies itself is a stronger lever on subjective
experience than how it gathers preferences. This aligns with the observation that XAI
effectiveness is not consistent across applications and requires domain-specific
evaluation \cite{rong2024}.

\subsection{Practical Implications}

For designers of conversational recommenders, three guidelines follow.

If the objective is trust, ground the justification in something the user can check ---
attributes or explicit comparisons --- even in a domain that feels emotional. The
temptation to humanise is strongest precisely where it appears most fitting, and that is
where it cost the most here.

If the objective is conversion, comparative justification was the strongest of the three
and was also among the strongest for trust. Notably, this reproduces an existing design
guideline derived from user-centric evaluation: explaining why an item is recommended
increases transparency, while explaining trade-offs increases persuasion \cite{pu2012}.
Our conditions A and C are close instances of those two prescriptions, and the outcome
pattern matches.

Most importantly, the two objectives are not served by the same design. This is a genuine
trade-off rather than a ranking, and a system optimising for conversion should not be
assumed to be optimising for trust.

One caveat guards against over-reading. Narrative justification was not judged useless:
usefulness and satisfaction were high in all three conditions and well above the scale
midpoint. Condition B underperformed specifically on trust, not globally.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

Several limitations qualify these conclusions.

\textit{The manipulation is broader than the explanation.} Each system prompt governs the
whole interaction, so the ten elicitation questions differed by condition alongside the
final justification. The study therefore manipulates justification logic as a coherent
whole and cannot separate the contribution of explanation content from that of
conversational framing. Disentangling the two requires a design that holds elicitation
constant while varying only the explanation.

\textit{Item equivalence across conditions was not verified.} Because the preference record
passed to the backend is produced by a dialogue that differs by condition, the recommended
products may not be distributed identically across conditions, and the distribution was
not checked against the session logs. The recommendation algorithm was invariant; the
recommended items are not demonstrated to be.

\textit{Measurement.} The two-item trust scale fell below the conventional reliability
threshold. Measurement error attenuates observed effects, so the large effect in H1 is
plausibly a conservative estimate and the weak association in H3 plausibly an
underestimate; nevertheless a multi-item instrument is required for confident inference.
Usefulness and satisfaction showed pronounced ceiling effects, limiting their
discriminating power.

\textit{Statistical approach.} The main analyses are rank-based while the moderation test
is parametric, an inconsistency adopted for want of an established rank-based test of a
two-factor interaction.

\textit{Design asymmetry.} The closing message in conditions A and C invited participants
to smell the fragrances at the tester area, whereas condition B contained no such
invitation. The resulting proportions did not differ significantly, but the asymmetry is a
flaw in the materials rather than a feature of the manipulation.

\textit{Generalisation.} Data come from one store, one brand, and 90 participants in a
single country, with age and gender unrecorded, so group equivalence could be verified
only on familiarity. Sensory access was near-universal because the tester area was open
throughout; conclusions therefore describe users who \textit{could} smell the product, and
may not transfer to purely online settings where that option is absent.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}

\subsection{Conclusion}

This paper asked whether the kind of justification a conversational recommender offers
changes how users respond to it, in a domain whose decisive attribute cannot be conveyed
through an interface. A between-subjects experiment with 90 participants, run in a
physical perfume store with an LLM-driven agent whose recommendation backend was held
constant, gives three answers.

Justification logic affects both perceived trust and purchase intention (RQ1), with a
large effect on the former and a medium-to-large effect on the latter. The direction is
the informative part: narrative justification, designed to be the warmest and most
personal, produced the lowest trust of the three conditions, while comparative
justification produced the highest purchase intention. Trust is weakly associated with
purchase intention (RQ2), and the structure of the results indicates that comparative
justification raises purchase intention through a route that does not pass through trust.
No evidence of moderation by perfume familiarity was found (RQ3), a result better read as
a limitation of statistical power than as evidence of absence.

The central lesson is that a product being hedonic does not make an emotional justification
the right one. What appears to govern the response is the nature of the user's task rather
than the nature of the product, and choosing a perfume through a recommender is an
evaluative task. A second lesson is that the two design objectives examined here are not
served by the same condition: trust was highest under feature-based and comparative-based
justification alike, whereas purchase intention was highest under comparative
justification alone. Designers of conversational recommenders in sensory domains face a
real trade-off, not a ranking.

\subsection{Future Work}

Four directions follow directly from the limitations.

\textit{Separate content from framing.} The manipulation here governed the whole
interaction. A design that holds preference elicitation constant while varying only the
final justification would isolate the contribution of explanation content, and would
establish whether the effect reported here originates in what the system says at the point
of recommendation or in how it has been speaking all along.

\textit{Measure trust dimensionally.} The mechanism proposed in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} makes a specific and testable prediction: narrative
justification should depress competence beliefs in particular, while leaving benevolence
beliefs intact or even raising them. An aggregate two-item scale cannot test this. Wang and
Benbasat's demonstration that different explanation types engage different trusting beliefs
\cite{wang2007} supplies the appropriate instrument design, and adopting it would convert
the present interpretation into a directly falsifiable claim.

\textit{Test hybrid justifications.} If narrative content undermines trust when it stands
alone, it does not follow that it undermines trust when it is attached to a factual base.
Feature-plus-narrative and comparison-plus-narrative conditions would establish whether
narrative functions as a complement rather than a substitute. Work that equips
conversational recommenders to capture a user's situation and emotional state from
dialogue \cite{yang2025} offers a natural foundation for such conditions, and interactive
explanation interfaces that let users scrutinise or request alternative explanatory
material \cite{ziegler2023} would allow the hybrid to be assembled by the user rather than
fixed by the designer.

\textit{Establish the boundary of the task-based account.} The proposal that task type
rather than product type governs the effect predicts that the advantage of
objective-grounded justification should weaken as the user's activity becomes more
experiential and less evaluative, and that the advantage of comparative justification
should shrink in domains whose decisive attributes can be transmitted digitally.
Replication across domains that differ in transmissibility, such as music and coffee,
alongside larger and familiarity-balanced samples adequate for interaction tests, would
put both predictions at risk. Finer-grained dialogue measures \cite{siro2023} would
complement the attitudinal instruments used here.
