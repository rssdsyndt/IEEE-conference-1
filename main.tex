\begin{abstract}
Explanations are widely assumed to increase user trust in AI-based recommender systems,
yet evidence indicates that the effect depends on the kind of explanation given rather
than on its presence. Comparative evidence on explanation types is scarce in
conversational recommender systems and almost absent in sensory-hedonic domains, where
the defining product attribute cannot be conveyed through a screen. This paper reports a
between-subjects experiment with 90 participants, 30 per condition, conducted in a
physical perfume store with an agent in which a large language model handles dialogue
while a knowledge-graph embedding backend selects the items. Three justification logics
were set by system-prompt conditioning without fine-tuning: feature-based,
narrative-based, and comparative-based. Justification logic significantly affected both
perceived trust and purchase intention, with a large and a medium-to-large effect
respectively. Contrary to the design intuition that warmer explanations build trust,
narrative justification produced the lowest trust, significantly below both
objective-grounded conditions, whereas comparative justification produced the highest
purchase intention. The association between trust and purchase intention was positive but
weak (Spearman rho = 0.229, p = 0.030), and no evidence of moderation by perfume
familiarity was found. Because trust was jointly highest under feature-based and
comparative justification while purchase intention was highest under comparative
justification alone, the two design objectives are not served by the same condition. The
results suggest that what governs the response is the evaluative nature of the user's
task rather than the hedonic nature of the product.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% ===============================================================

Conversational recommender systems (CRS) support a multi-turn dialogue in which the system
elicits preferences, explains its suggestions, and processes feedback \cite{jannach2021}.
Large language models (LLMs) have made such systems inexpensive to build, sharpening a
question that predates them: when a recommender explains itself, what should it say?

Explainable AI (XAI) is positioned as the bridge between opaque models and users deciding
how far to rely on them \cite{rong2024}. The canonical framing holds that explanations
serve seven aims --- effectiveness, satisfaction, transparency, scrutability, trust,
persuasiveness, efficiency --- and that these can be incompatible, so an explanation
optimised for one may degrade another \cite{tintarev2012}. Consumer research supports the
optimistic half: across four studies, post hoc explanations raised interpretability
perceptions and thence trust, purchase intention, and click-through \cite{chen2024}. The
pessimistic half receives less attention. With 215 participants, feature-importance
explanations produced higher trust than counterfactual ones, and explanations raised trust
even when the system performed poorly, which the authors read as overreliance rather than
warranted trust \cite{brito2023}; explanation interfaces can likewise induce trust
calibration errors \cite{naiseh2021}. The question therefore shifts from whether an
explanation is present to which kind is given.

Two gaps follow. \textit{First, explanation types are seldom compared against one another
inside a single conversational system.} The foundational demonstration that explanation
type determines which component of trust is affected --- \textit{how} raising competence
and benevolence, \textit{why} raising benevolence, \textit{trade-off} raising integrity ---
used a static recommendation agent \cite{wang2007}. Later work contrasts
feature-importance with counterfactual explanations \cite{brito2023} or organises
contrastive generation methods into a taxonomy \cite{stepin2021}, but outside
conversational e-commerce.

\textit{Second, sensory-hedonic domains remain underexplored.} The effect of explanation is
not domain invariant: it is stronger in utilitarian than hedonic domains, and explanation
type interacts with domain, attribute-based explanations suiting utilitarian settings and
user-based ones hedonic settings \cite{chen2024}. Negative reviews likewise depress
purchase for utilitarian but not hedonic products, and not for taste-related factors
\cite{varga2024}, and product type moderates the path from perceived deception to
repurchase intention \cite{wangx2022}. Findings from rational domains cannot be assumed to
transfer. Perfume is an extreme case: its defining attribute cannot be transmitted
digitally at all, its vocabulary is mastered mainly by enthusiasts, and online purchase
uncertainty is correspondingly high.

Three questions follow. \textbf{RQ1:} Does justification logic affect perceived trust and
purchase intention? \textbf{RQ2:} Is trust associated with purchase intention?
\textbf{RQ3:} Does familiarity moderate the effect?

This paper contributes a perfume CRS in which an LLM handles language while a
knowledge-graph embedding backend selects items, with justification logic switched by
system-prompt conditioning; comparative evidence in a domain whose core attribute is not
digitally transmissible, responding to the call for domain-sensitive XAI evidence
\cite{chen2024}; a counter-intuitive result, that narrative justification produced the
\textit{lowest} trust, consistent with evidence that competent conversational styles
outperform warm ones on cognition-oriented tasks \cite{libaoku2024}; an explicit design
trade-off, since the conditions maximising trust and purchase intention differ; and
transparently reported null and weak results.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% ===============================================================

\subsection{Explanation Type in XAI and Recommender Systems}

Tintarev and Masthoff established the reference taxonomy of explanatory aims and showed
that personalising feature-based explanations was \textit{detrimental} to effectiveness
while plausibly improving satisfaction, contrary to their own hypothesis
\cite{tintarev2012}. Wang and Benbasat demonstrated that explanation type, not mere
presence, determines which trusting belief is engaged \cite{wang2007}. Later work added a
taxonomy of contrastive and counterfactual generation methods \cite{stepin2021}, evidence
that feature-importance outperforms counterfactual explanation for non-experts while also
raising trust in an unreliable system \cite{brito2023}, and design principles aimed at
trust calibration rather than trust maximisation \cite{naiseh2021}. A review of 97 user
studies concludes that XAI effectiveness is \textit{not consistent across applications}
\cite{rong2024}, and domain studies accordingly measure perception directly against
transparency, satisfaction, trust, and scrutability \cite{decampos2024}. The anchor here is
Chen et al., whose four studies establish that post hoc explanations raise interpretability
and thence trust, that the effect is stronger in utilitarian domains, and that
attribute-based explanations suit utilitarian domains while user-based ones suit hedonic
domains \cite{chen2024}.

\subsection{Explanations in Conversational Recommender Systems}

Jannach et al. observe that although explanations are considered central to a convincing
dialogue, the topic is not explored a great deal \cite{jannach2021}. Since then,
interactive conversational explanation has been shown to improve user evaluation when
users can scrutinise explanatory arguments \cite{ziegler2023}; dialogue aspects shaping
satisfaction have been annotated, with relevance dominant at turn level \cite{siro2023};
and the choice of recommendation algorithm has been shown to alter critiquing behaviour
across 228 participants \cite{rana2024}. The closest methodological parallel built three
CRS versions and ran a between-subjects experiment with 66 participants, finding \textit{no}
significant effect on any questionnaire-based construct, though objective measures differed
\cite{ziegfeld2025}; that manipulation concerned preference elicitation rather than
explanation content.

\subsection{Trust, Purchase Intention, and Hedonic Domains}

Trust in an artefact is conventionally decomposed into competence, benevolence, and
integrity \cite{mcknight2002}; user-centric evaluation supplies validated constructs and
the guidance that explaining \textit{why} increases transparency while explaining
\textit{trade-offs} increases persuasion \cite{pu2012}; usefulness follows the technology
acceptance tradition \cite{davis1989}. The trust-to-purchase chain has been validated for
chatbots using questionnaires and EEG, though media richness and playfulness did
\textit{not} raise trust, the authors reasoning that shopping conversation is a functional
task \cite{yen2021}. Anthropomorphism qualifies the chain: warmth and competence both raise
trust, but exchange relationships strengthen the weight of competence \cite{cheng2022};
both feed trust and chatbot overload, so trust is not the sole channel to purchase
\cite{liyang2025}; and anthropomorphism can backfire through expectancy violation
\cite{crolic2022}. Most directly, no interaction style is universally superior: competent
styles win cognition-oriented tasks and warm styles emotion-oriented ones, mediated
respectively by agent-mind and experience-mind perception \cite{libaoku2024}. Hedonic
evaluation is subjective and unstable relative to utilitarian evaluation \cite{wangx2022},
\cite{varga2024}, and familiarity is a plausible boundary condition: expertise reduces
trust in explainable decision support and moderates the effect of explanation on trusting
intention \cite{bayer2023}, an interaction requiring four experiments and 731 participants
to characterise \cite{celar2023}.

This study differs from its nearest antecedents on three axes at once: explanation types
are compared against one another rather than against a no-explanation baseline; the
comparison occurs inside a multi-turn LLM-driven conversation rather than a static display
\cite{wang2007}, \cite{chen2024}; and the domain is sensory-hedonic with a
non-transmissible core attribute.

\section{System Design}
\label{sec:system}
% ===============================================================

\subsection{Architecture}

The platform is a conversational perfume recommender deployed as a web application and
operated on site in a physical perfume store. GPT-4o-mini conducts the dialogue, extracts
preferences, and verbalises the justification, while a conversational-recommendation hybrid
knowledge graph embedding (CR-HKGE) backend selects the items (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig_architecture}}
\caption{System architecture. Item selection is delegated to the backend, whose scoring is
identical in every condition; the condition is set once, at the system-prompt layer, and
governs both elicitation and justification.}
\label{fig:arch}
\end{figure}

Two properties matter. First, \textit{the backend is a knowledge graph embedding model, not
a retrieval-augmented generation pipeline}: candidates are scored in a learned embedding
space over a graph of 998 entities and 9,255 triples across seven relation types,
comprising 340 catalogue products, 312 catalogue-side attribute entities (17 olfactory
families, 110 accords, 185 visual notes), and a cross-reference layer of 346 entities.
Selecting three items from 340 is a non-trivial retrieval problem. The design follows
knowledge-graph recommenders that aggregate collaborative and semantic signals through
graph attention \cite{litois2023}, \cite{elahi2024}, including work extending such graphs
to conversational settings to capture a user's situation and emotional state from dialogue
\cite{yang2025}. Second, \textit{the LLM is an interface, not the recommender}, mirroring
the pattern that employs LLMs as the brain and recommender models as tools
\cite{huang2023}, motivated by the observation that LLMs lack knowledge of domain-specific
catalogues and propose products that do not exist. The recommendation \textit{algorithm} is
therefore invariant across conditions; the recommended \textit{items} are not guaranteed to
be, because the backend is queried with a preference record the dialogue itself produces.

\subsection{Experimental Conditions}

The condition is fixed by system-prompt conditioning at the start of each session, without
fine-tuning, following the prompting paradigm by which LLMs are adapted to recommendation
tasks \cite{zhao2024}. The manipulated variable is the \textit{justification logic}, and it
governs the whole interaction: each prompt instructs the agent to hold its assigned logic
``from your first greeting to your final closing message,'' so the ten elicitation
questions are framed differently in each condition, as is the final explanation
(Table~\ref{tab:conditions}). This is deliberate --- a system whose framing contradicted
its own explanations would be incoherent --- but its cost is that explanation content
cannot be separated from conversational framing.

The contrast is visible in how the logics render the same product. Condition A produced
``\textit{it belongs to the aquatic family with green and fresh-spicy as its main
character --- exactly what you are looking for in an office setting}''; condition B,
``\textit{picture a fresh scent flowing gently, like morning dew, giving you new energy and
confidence}''; condition C, ``\textit{it is the freshest and brightest of the three --- it
foregrounds freshness with a woody touch}''. Condition C transcripts consistently name one
or two co-recommended products as explicit comparators.

Conditions A and C have established counterparts: attribute-based explanation, which yields
higher trust than counterfactual explanation for non-experts \cite{brito2023}, and
contrastive explanation \cite{stepin2021}, which improves the accuracy of users' own
decisions \cite{celar2023}. Condition B is the novel element. The literature examines
conversational \textit{style} on the warmth-competence axis \cite{libaoku2024},
\cite{cheng2022} while treating explanation \textit{content} as fixed; condition B makes
affective content the substance of the justification, supplying no compositional facts and
no comparative frame. It is closest to the user-based explanations found more effective in
hedonic domains \cite{chen2024}, differing in one respect examined later: user-based
explanations carry social proof, purely narrative ones carry none. One asymmetry is
recorded because it bears on a later analysis: the closing messages in conditions A and C
invite the user to smell the fragrances at the tester area, whereas condition B does not.

\subsection{Conversational Interface}

A session proceeds through greeting and consent, elicitation, presentation of three ranked
perfumes with the condition-appropriate explanation, and the questionnaire. Elicitation
follows a fixed protocol of exactly ten questions, one per message: in the terms of
Ziegfeld et al. \cite{ziegfeld2025} this is the \textit{high-guidance, low-restriction}
point of the elicitation space, since the system fixes the topic sequence while answers are
free text. The first question records familiarity through three selectable tags. The
dialogue yields a preference record of familiarity, occasion, primary and secondary scent
character, intensity, attributes to avoid, and free-text context; fields are identical
across conditions but expressed in condition-specific vocabulary. Sessions comprised a mean
of 20.29 messages ($SD = 4.67$, range 9--55), with means of 20.60 in A, 19.20 in B, and
21.07 in C, so interaction length is an unlikely alternative explanation for the
differences below.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}
% ===============================================================

\subsection{Design and Participants}

The study uses a single-factor between-subjects design with three levels of justification
logic, assignment by session counter, and 30 participants per condition. A between-subjects
design was preferred deliberately: had participants experienced all three logics the
comparison would have become explicit, inviting demand characteristics and carryover. The
cost is reduced power for the interaction test.

Familiarity was recorded at the first elicitation question and recruitment was
quota-balanced across its three levels: 30 beginners, 30 enthusiasts, 30 collectors. For
analysis these were dichotomised, beginners coded \textit{novice} ($n = 30$), enthusiasts
with collectors coded \textit{enthusiast} ($n = 60$). The dichotomisation rests on construct
grounds: what separates the groups is command of olfactory vocabulary, since enthusiasts
and collectors alike understand terms such as accord and sillage while beginners do not,
and condition A rests entirely on that vocabulary. The two-to-one imbalance is therefore a
consequence of the coding decision, not of recruitment; but the novice cells are small ---
10, 12, and 8 in A, B, and C, against 20, 18, and 22 enthusiasts. Familiarity was
nonetheless evenly distributed, $\chi^{2}(2) = 1.200$, $p = .549$, Cram\'{e}r's
$V = 0.116$, all expected counts at or above 10.

Age and gender were not recorded. Participation was voluntary and preceded by an on-screen
consent notice stating that the system formed part of collaborative academic research at
the authors' institution, that responses would be fully anonymised, that data would be used
for academic purposes only, and that participation could be declined. No personally
identifying information was collected, responses were anonymised at capture, and no
compensation was offered. The study involved no deception beyond withholding the research
hypothesis, no sensitive data, and no vulnerable population. It was not submitted for
formal institutional ethics review.

\subsection{Measures and Procedure}

Four constructs were measured with two five-point Likert items each. Trust asked whether
the three recommended perfumes matched the participant's preferences overall and whether
the explanation convinced them the system understood their needs, adapted from the
trusting-beliefs tradition \cite{mcknight2002}. Purchase intention asked about interest in
buying one of the recommended perfumes after reading the explanation and the likelihood of
considering a purchase after the session; satisfaction asked about overall satisfaction and
willingness to recommend the agent; both were adapted from the ResQue framework
\cite{pu2012}. Usefulness asked whether the explanation helped the participant understand
why the perfume suited them and whether it supported the purchase decision
\cite{davis1989}. A ninth, binary item recorded whether the participant had already smelled
the recommended product; it measures \textit{sensory access already obtained}, not an
intention to obtain it, and is treated as exploratory. All items were administered in
Indonesian.

The study ran offline in a physical perfume store during normal operating sessions.
Participants read the consent notice, worked through the ten-question sequence until three
ranked recommendations and their explanations were delivered, and completed the
questionnaire; the consent screen advertised approximately ten minutes. Because the tester
area remained accessible, participants could smell the fragrances physically.
Participation arose from an institutional collaboration with the store rather than a
recruitment campaign, and participants were not told that justification logic was under
investigation.

\subsection{Manipulation Check and Analysis Plan}

To verify that conditions were distinguishable from transcripts alone, 30 transcripts, 10
per condition, were coded by three sources: the system-recorded condition
(\textit{actual}), the researcher (\textit{self}), and an independent rater blind to
assignment (\textit{rater}). Agreement was quantified with Cohen's kappa against the
benchmarks of Landis and Koch, who assign 0.61--0.80 to substantial and 0.81--1.00 to
almost perfect agreement while noting the divisions are arbitrary \cite{landis1977}.
Because self-coding was not blind, evidentiary weight rests on the independent rater.

Cronbach's alpha is downward-biased for two-item scales, so reliability was estimated with
the Spearman-Brown coefficient, the estimator examined for such scales in
\cite{eisinga2013}. Shapiro-Wilk tests indicated departures from normality in several cells
and Levene's test heterogeneity of variance for trust, so rank-based procedures were
adopted: Kruskal-Wallis, Dunn's test with Holm correction, Spearman's rho, and Wilcoxon
signed-rank. Effect size was computed as $\varepsilon^{2} = H/(n-1)$, with rank-biserial
correlations for pairwise comparisons. The moderation hypothesis used a two-way analysis of
variance, an acknowledged inconsistency adopted for want of an established rank-based test
of a two-factor interaction. Analyses used JASP v0.97.1.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
% ===============================================================

\subsection{Manipulation Check and Reliability}

The independent rater reproduced the system-recorded condition with $\kappa = 0.900$
($SE = 0.068$, 95\% CI [0.767, 1.000]) and agreed with the researcher's coding at the same
level; the researcher's own, non-blind coding matched ground truth exactly. Both
disagreements occurred in condition B, where the rater classified narrative transcripts as
feature-based. The manipulation was therefore not perfectly pure, and its impurity ran
towards condition A --- a direction that works \textit{against} the effect reported below,
making that effect a conservative estimate.

Spearman-Brown coefficients were 0.801 for purchase intention (95\% CI [0.551, 1.050],
average inter-item $r = 0.670$), 0.731 for usefulness ([0.449, 1.013], $r = 0.593$), 0.689
for satisfaction ([0.413, 0.966], $r = 0.532$), and 0.624 for trust ([0.306, 0.941],
$r = 0.454$). The trust figure is below the conventional 0.70 threshold and reported
unadjusted. Two features qualify it: the interval is wide, reflecting the precision
obtainable from two items and 90 observations, and an inter-item correlation of 0.454 lies
within the range normally considered healthy for a narrow construct, indicating the
constraint is scale length rather than item content.

\subsection{Descriptive Statistics and Assumption Checks}

Trust was highest in condition C ($M = 4.65$, $SD = 0.35$) and lowest in B ($M = 4.05$,
$SD = 0.64$), with A intermediate ($M = 4.48$, $SD = 0.46$); medians were 4.50, 4.00, and
4.50. Purchase intention was highest in C ($M = 4.23$, $SD = 0.52$) against A
($M = 3.78$, $SD = 0.77$) and B ($M = 3.55$, $SD = 0.84$). Usefulness and satisfaction were
uniformly high, with means between 4.33 and 4.85 and medians of 5.00 in most cells.

Shapiro-Wilk tests rejected normality in nine of the twelve construct-by-condition cells,
including the trust cells in A and C ($W = 0.854$, $W = 0.781$, both $p < .001$) and every
usefulness and satisfaction cell. Levene's test indicated heterogeneity of variance for
trust, $F(2, 87) = 3.292$, $p = .042$, but not for purchase intention,
$F(2, 87) = 2.538$, $p = .085$. Usefulness and satisfaction showed pronounced ceiling
effects.

\subsection{Hypothesis Testing}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig_means}}

\label{fig:means}

\textbf{H1.} Condition affected perceived trust, $H(2) = 16.57$, $p < .001$,
$\varepsilon^{2} = 0.186$, a large effect. Dunn's test located the difference: B scored
below A ($z = 2.713$, $p = .013$, $r_{rb} = -0.397$) and below C ($z = -3.985$,
$p < .001$, $r_{rb} = 0.564$), while A and C did not differ ($z = -1.272$, $p = .203$). H1
is supported, but the direction reverses the intuition that motivated condition B: the
warmest justification was trusted least.

\textbf{H2.} Condition also affected purchase intention, $H(2) = 11.88$, $p = .003$,
$\varepsilon^{2} = 0.133$. Here C scored above A ($z = -2.326$, $p = .040$,
$r_{rb} = 0.343$) and above B ($z = -3.365$, $p = .002$, $r_{rb} = 0.492$), while A and B
did not differ ($z = 1.039$, $p = .299$). The two patterns do not coincide: trust was
jointly highest in A and C, purchase intention highest in C alone.

\textbf{H3.} Trust and purchase intention were positively but weakly associated,
$\rho = 0.229$, $p = .030$, 95\% CI [0.025, 0.437] from 1{,}000 bootstrap replicates; the
parametric counterpart did not reach significance, $r = 0.161$, $p = .129$. Trust accounts
for roughly five per cent of the variance in purchase intention.

\textbf{H4.} A two-way analysis of variance crossing condition with dichotomised
familiarity produced no evidence of interaction, $F(2, 84) = 0.452$, $p = .638$. The
condition main effect persisted, $F(2, 84) = 10.57$, $p < .001$; familiarity had none,
$F(1, 84) = 0.039$, $p = .845$. Given novice cells of 10, 12, and 8 and a significant
Levene test for this design, $F(5, 84) = 3.153$, $p = .012$, this is better read as a
limitation of power than as evidence of absence.

Two supplementary analyses were run. Usefulness and satisfaction both exceeded 3.5
decisively (Wilcoxon: $V = 3909$ and $V = 3533$, both $p < .001$, rank-biserial 0.952 and
0.933). This is an interpretive safeguard: participants judged the system useful and
satisfying in every condition, so the trust differences cannot be attributed to one
condition being served by a system that simply worked less well. Separately, 77 of 90
participants (85.6\%) had already smelled the recommended product, a proportion that did
not differ significantly across conditions, $\chi^{2}(2) = 5.035$, $p = .081$.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ===============================================================

\subsection{Why Narrative Justification Lowered Trust (RQ1)}

Justification logic affected trust with a large effect, in the direction opposite to the
intuition that motivated condition B. The warmest justification was trusted least. This is
stated without hedging because it is the study's principal contribution.

The most economical account is that emotional narrative weakens the signal of competence.
Li et al. show that no anthropomorphic style is universally superior: competent styles
produce more positive responses on cognition-oriented tasks, mediated by agent-mind
perception, whereas warm styles prevail on emotion-oriented tasks, mediated by
experience-mind perception \cite{libaoku2024}. The decisive variable is the nature of the
\textit{task}, not of the \textit{product}. Perfume is hedonic as a product, but selecting
one through a recommender is an evaluative task: the user weighs options, assesses fit, and
decides. On that reading the observed ordering is what the mind-perception account
predicts.

Two results converge. Exchange relationships strengthen the importance of perceived
competence on trust, while communal relationships do not amplify warmth \cite{cheng2022},
and an in-store purchase consultation is an exchange relationship in the plainest sense.
Yen and Chiang found that neither media richness nor playfulness raised trust in shopping
chatbots, reasoning that such communication is a functional task \cite{yen2021}; the
present result extends that logic from conversational decoration to the substance of the
justification. That anthropomorphic warmth can backfire outright, through expectancy
violations caused by inflated efficacy expectations, is separately established
\cite{crolic2022}.

The finding stands in apparent tension with the anchor study, which reports user-based
explanations as more effective than attribute-based ones in hedonic domains
\cite{chen2024}. Two differences reconcile the accounts. Their explanations accompany a
static display, whereas ours are sustained across a ten-question dialogue; in conversation
the user accumulates evidence about the system's competence, not only about the product,
and a narrative that reads as evocative once can read as evasive over twenty messages.
Further, the two are not the same object: user-based explanation carries social proof,
purely narrative explanation carries no evidence at all. Condition B isolates affect from
evidence in a way user-based explanation does not. Seen thus the result is less exotic than
it appears: personalising explanations has previously been found detrimental to
effectiveness even while improving satisfaction, contrary to the authors' own hypothesis
\cite{tintarev2012}.

Feature-based and comparative justification did not differ on trust. Both anchor the
recommendation in something checkable --- composition in one case, position relative to
named alternatives in the other --- so either displays command of the domain, which is what
an evaluative task rewards. This accords with feature-importance explanations outperforming
counterfactual ones for non-experts \cite{brito2023} and with the premise that explanation
type determines which trusting belief is engaged \cite{wang2007}.

\subsection{A Thin Bridge to Purchase Intention (RQ2)}

The association between trust and purchase intention was weak and the parametric
coefficient non-significant, so no causal claim is made. The more informative evidence is
structural: trust was jointly highest in A and C, whereas purchase intention was highest in
C alone. Were trust the principal route, A should have followed C upward. It did not.
Comparative justification therefore appears to act on purchase intention through a channel
that bypasses trust, most plausibly by reducing decision uncertainty at the point of
choice. Celar and Byrne provide direct support: counterfactual explanations improved the
accuracy of participants' \textit{own} decisions more than causal ones \cite{celar2023}.
Contrastive information helps people decide for themselves, which is what is needed when
the decisive attribute cannot be conveyed by the interface. This is consistent with models
treating trust as one channel among several \cite{liyang2025} and qualifies the
trust-centric reading of the chatbot literature \cite{yen2021}; maximal trust is in any
case not the goal, since explanations can produce undeliberated agreement
\cite{naiseh2021}.

\subsection{No Evidence of Moderation (RQ3)}

The interaction was far from significant, and this is an absence of evidence rather than
evidence of absence. The grounds for expecting moderation were strong: domain expertise
reduces trust in explainable decision support, and the effect of explanation on trusting
intention is itself moderated by expertise \cite{bayer2023}. Two features limit the
conclusion. The novice cells held 10, 12, and 8 participants, too few to detect a plausible
interaction, whereas characterising how explanation type interacts with familiarity
required four experiments and 731 participants \cite{celar2023}. Dichotomising three
familiarity levels may also have blunted a gradient.

\subsection{Implications}

Three theoretical implications follow. First, and most substantively, the classification
governing explanation design may be the type of \textit{task} rather than the type of
\textit{product}. The literature partitions domains into utilitarian and hedonic
\cite{chen2024}, \cite{wangx2022}, \cite{varga2024}; these results suggest a cross-cutting
axis, whether the user's immediate activity is evaluative or experiential. Perfume is
hedonic on the first axis and evaluative on the second, and the second predicted the
outcome \cite{libaoku2024}. Second, the results give concrete empirical support to the
claim that explanatory aims can be incompatible \cite{tintarev2012}: the conditions
maximising trust and purchase intention differed. Third, a comparable three-condition CRS
study found no significant effect on any subjective construct while manipulating preference
elicitation \cite{ziegfeld2025}; the contrast suggests, without establishing, that
justification logic is a stronger lever on subjective experience than elicitation method,
consistent with the observation that XAI effectiveness varies across applications
\cite{rong2024}.

Practically, if the objective is trust, ground the justification in something checkable
even in a domain that feels emotional; the temptation to humanise is strongest exactly
where it proved most costly here. If the objective is conversion, comparative justification
was strongest, reproducing an existing guideline that explaining why increases transparency
while explaining trade-offs increases persuasion \cite{pu2012}. Crucially the two
objectives are not served by the same design. One caveat guards against over-reading:
usefulness and satisfaction were high in all conditions, so condition B underperformed
specifically on trust, not globally.

\subsection{Limitations}

\textit{The manipulation is broader than the explanation.} Each prompt governs the whole
interaction, so elicitation differed alongside justification; separating the two requires a
design holding elicitation constant. \textit{Item equivalence was not verified.} Because
the preference record is produced by a dialogue that differs by condition, recommended
products may not be distributed identically, and this was not checked against session logs.
\textit{Measurement.} The two-item trust scale fell below the conventional threshold;
measurement error attenuates effects, so H1 is plausibly conservative and H3 plausibly an
underestimate, but a multi-item instrument is needed. Usefulness and satisfaction showed
ceiling effects. \textit{Statistical approach.} Main analyses are rank-based while the
moderation test is parametric. \textit{Design asymmetry.} Conditions A and C invited
participants to the tester area and B did not; the resulting proportions did not differ
significantly, but this is a flaw in the materials. \textit{Generalisation.} Data come from
one store, one brand, and 90 participants in a single country, with age and gender
unrecorded. Sensory access was near-universal, so conclusions describe users who
\textit{could} smell the product and may not transfer to purely online settings.

% ===============================================================
\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ===============================================================

Justification logic affects both perceived trust and purchase intention (RQ1), with a large
and a medium-to-large effect. The direction is the informative part: narrative
justification, designed to be the warmest, produced the lowest trust, while comparative
justification produced the highest purchase intention. Trust is only weakly associated with
purchase intention (RQ2), and the structure of the results indicates that comparative
justification raises purchase intention through a route bypassing trust. No evidence of
moderation by familiarity was found (RQ3), better read as a limitation of power. The
central lesson is that a product being hedonic does not make an emotional justification the
right one; what governs the response appears to be the nature of the user's task. The
second is that the two design objectives are not served by the same condition, so designers
in sensory domains face a trade-off rather than a ranking.

Four directions follow from the limitations. Holding elicitation constant while varying only
the final justification would separate explanation content from conversational framing.
Measuring trust dimensionally would convert the proposed mechanism into a falsifiable
prediction, that narrative justification depresses competence beliefs specifically while
leaving benevolence intact; the demonstration that explanation types engage different
trusting beliefs \cite{wang2007} supplies the instrument design. Hybrid conditions would
establish whether narrative functions as a complement rather than a substitute when
attached to a factual base, for which conversational recommenders that capture situation
and emotional state from dialogue \cite{yang2025} offer a foundation and interactive
explanation interfaces \cite{ziegler2023} a means of letting users assemble the hybrid
themselves. Finally, the task-based account predicts that the advantage of
objective-grounded justification should weaken as the activity becomes more experiential,
and that the advantage of comparative justification should shrink where decisive attributes
are digitally transmissible; replication across domains differing in transmissibility, with
larger familiarity-balanced samples and finer-grained dialogue measures \cite{siro2023},
would put both predictions at risk.
