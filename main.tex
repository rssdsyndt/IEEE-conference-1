\begin{abstract}
Explanations are widely assumed to increase user trust in AI-based recommender
systems, yet accumulating evidence indicates that the effect depends on the kind
of explanation provided rather than on its mere presence. Comparative evidence on
explanation types remains scarce in conversational recommender systems, and is
almost absent in sensory-hedonic domains, where the defining product attribute
cannot be transmitted through a screen. This study reports a between-subjects
experiment with 90 participants, 30 per condition, using a conversational
recommender system for perfume in which a large language model handles dialogue
while a knowledge-graph embedding backend produces the recommendations.
Explanation type was manipulated purely through system-prompt conditioning, with
no fine-tuning, across three conditions: feature-based, narrative-based, and
comparative-based. Perceived trust and purchase intention were measured with
adapted two-item scales and analysed using Kruskal-Wallis and Dunn tests.
Explanation type significantly affected both trust and purchase intention, with a
large and a medium-to-large effect size respectively. Contrary to the common
design intuition that warmer and more relatable explanations build trust,
narrative-based explanations produced the lowest trust, significantly below both
objective-grounded conditions. Comparative-based explanations produced the highest
purchase intention, significantly above the other two conditions. The association
between trust and purchase intention was positive but weak (Spearman rho = 0.229,
p = 0.030), and no evidence of moderation by perfume familiarity was found. The
two design goals were therefore not achieved by the same condition, which
indicates a concrete design trade-off for conversational recommender systems in
hedonic domains.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% ===============================================================

Conversational recommender systems (CRS) support a task-oriented, multi-turn dialogue
in which the system elicits a user's current preferences, provides explanations for its
suggestions, and processes feedback \cite{jannach2021}. Large language models (LLMs)
have made such systems inexpensive to build and natural to converse with, which sharpens
a design question that predates them: when a recommender explains itself, what should it
actually say?

Explainable AI (XAI) is widely positioned as the bridge between opaque recommendation
models and users who must decide how far to rely on them; a systematic review of 97 user
studies of model explanations organises the field around trust, understanding, usability,
and human-AI collaboration performance \cite{rong2024}. Within recommender systems
specifically, the canonical framing holds that explanations may serve seven aims ---
effectiveness, satisfaction, transparency, scrutability, trust, persuasiveness, and
efficiency --- and, critically, that these aims can be incompatible, so that an
explanation optimised for one may degrade another \cite{tintarev2012}. Consumer research
supports the optimistic half of this framing: across four studies, the presence of post
hoc explanations raised interpretability perceptions and, through them, trust, purchase
intention, and click-through on AI recommendations \cite{chen2024}.

The pessimistic half receives less attention in practice. In an experiment with 215
participants, feature-importance explanations produced higher trust than counterfactual
explanations, and explanations raised trust even when the underlying system performed
poorly, a pattern the authors read as overreliance rather than warranted trust
\cite{brito2023}. Qualitative work on explanation interfaces reaches a compatible
conclusion, documenting trust calibration errors in which users agree or disagree with a
recommendation without deliberation \cite{naiseh2021}. Together, these results move the
question from whether an explanation is present to which kind of explanation is given.

Two gaps follow, and this paper addresses both.

\textit{First, explanation types are seldom compared against one another inside a single
conversational system.} The foundational demonstration that explanation type determines
which component of trust is affected --- \textit{how} explanations raising competence and
benevolence beliefs, \textit{why} explanations raising benevolence, and
\textit{trade-off} explanations raising integrity --- was obtained on a static
recommendation agent \cite{wang2007}. More recent comparative work contrasts
feature-importance with counterfactual explanations \cite{brito2023}, or organises
contrastive and counterfactual generation methods into a taxonomy \cite{stepin2021}, but
does so outside conversational e-commerce. A head-to-head comparison of feature-based,
narrative-based, and comparative-based justification within one LLM-driven CRS has not,
to the authors' knowledge, been reported.

\textit{Second, sensory-hedonic domains remain underexplored relative to utilitarian
ones}, and the difference is consequential rather than cosmetic. The facilitating effect
of post hoc explanations is stronger in the utilitarian than in the hedonic
decision-making domain, and explanation type interacts with domain: attribute-based
explanations are more effective at enhancing trust in utilitarian settings, whereas
user-based explanations are more effective in hedonic ones \cite{chen2024}. Clickstream
evidence converges from a different angle, showing that negative reviews reduce purchase
probability and increase search for substitutes for utilitarian products but not for
hedonic ones, and for reviews concerning product functionality or customer service but
not for taste-related factors \cite{varga2024}. Product type similarly moderates the path
from perceived deception to online repurchase intention, with hedonic purchases proving
less sensitive \cite{wangx2022}. Findings obtained in rational domains therefore cannot
be assumed to transfer.

Perfume is an extreme case of the hedonic condition. Its defining attribute cannot be
transmitted through a screen at all, unlike the visual attributes of clothing or the
auditory attributes of music. Its descriptive vocabulary, comprising terms such as
accord, sillage, and olfactory family, is commanded mainly by enthusiasts, which makes
domain familiarity a plausible moderator of how a feature-based justification is
received. The resulting purchase uncertainty is correspondingly high. If explanation
design has domain-dependent effects, perfume is where they should be most visible.

Accordingly, this study asks three questions. \textbf{RQ1}: does the type of
justification a conversational recommender offers affect perceived trust and purchase
intention? \textbf{RQ2}: is perceived trust associated with purchase intention?
\textbf{RQ3}: does perfume familiarity moderate the effect of justification type?

The contributions are as follows.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{An artefact.} A perfume CRS in which an LLM conducts the dialogue and a
knowledge graph embedding backend selects the items, and in which three justification
logics are instantiated by system-prompt conditioning alone, without fine-tuning.
Because item selection is delegated to a backend whose scoring procedure is identical in
every condition, the manipulation operates on how the system reasons aloud rather than
on the recommendation procedure itself.

\item \textbf{Comparative evidence from a hedonic domain.} A between-subjects field
experiment with 90 participants, conducted in a physical perfume store where the
recommended fragrances could actually be smelled, establishes that justification type
significantly affects both perceived trust and purchase intention. This responds directly
to the call for domain-sensitive evidence raised in \cite{chen2024}.

\item \textbf{A counter-intuitive result with theoretical grounding.} Narrative-based
justification, designed to be the warmest and most relatable of the three, produced the
\textit{lowest} trust. The result inverts a prevailing design heuristic and is consistent
with evidence that a competent conversational style outperforms a warm one on
cognition-oriented tasks, mediated by agent-mind perception, while the reverse holds for
emotion-oriented tasks \cite{libaoku2024}.

\item \textbf{An explicit design trade-off.} The condition that maximised trust and the
condition that maximised purchase intention were not the same, and the association
between the two outcomes proved weak. This supplies empirical support for the claim that
explanatory aims can conflict \cite{tintarev2012}, and indicates that comparative
justification reaches purchase intention by a route that does not pass through trust.

\item \textbf{Transparent treatment of null and weak results.} No evidence of moderation
by familiarity was found, and the constraints that qualify this conclusion, together with
those affecting a supplementary behavioural measure, are reported rather than suppressed.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews
explanation types in XAI and recommender systems, explanations in CRS, trust and purchase
intention, hedonic product domains, and familiarity as a boundary condition.
Section~\ref{sec:system} describes the system and the three conditions.
Section~\ref{sec:method} presents the method. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the
results, Section~\ref{sec:discussion} interprets them and states the limitations, and
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% ===============================================================

\subsection{Explanation Types in XAI and Recommender Systems}

Tintarev and Masthoff established the reference framing for explanation in recommender
systems, in which explanations may serve seven aims and any evaluation must state which
aim it targets because the aims can be incompatible \cite{tintarev2012}. Their own
empirical studies illustrate the tension concretely: across two domains, personalising
feature-based explanations proved detrimental to effectiveness even though it may improve
user satisfaction, a result the authors report as contrary to their hypothesis. The
structural lesson --- that a more appealing explanation can be a less useful one --- bears
directly on the present study.

Wang and Benbasat supplied the foundational demonstration that it is explanation
\textit{type}, not explanation presence, that determines which component of trust is
affected \cite{wang2007}. In a laboratory experiment with a purpose-built recommendation
agent, the use of \textit{how} explanations increased competence and benevolence beliefs,
\textit{why} explanations increased benevolence beliefs, and \textit{trade-off}
explanations increased integrity beliefs. Their conclusion, that the effects of
explanations depend largely on the contents and types of explanations provided, is the
premise on which this study rests.

Subsequent work has sharpened the picture without settling it. Stepin et al. conducted a
systematic review of contrastive and counterfactual explanation and proposed a taxonomy
covering both theoretical accounts and computational frameworks \cite{stepin2021}. De
Brito Duarte et al. compared feature-importance against counterfactual explanations with
215 participants and found trust higher under feature importance, while also observing
that explanations increased trust even when the system was unreliable; they further note
that other work suggests counterfactual explanations may be particularly advantageous
where expert knowledge is available \cite{brito2023}. Naiseh et al. approach the same
tension from the design side, deriving five principles intended to support calibrated
rather than maximal trust \cite{naiseh2021}.

Two broader observations frame these results. Reviewing 97 user studies of model
explanations, Rong et al. concluded that the effectiveness of XAI in users' interaction
with models is not consistent across applications, and that such evaluations rarely draw
on cognitive or social science \cite{rong2024}. Domain-specific studies accordingly
measure perception directly; de Campos et al., for instance, evaluate an explainable
content-based recommender against transparency, satisfaction, trust, and scrutability in
a user study \cite{decampos2024}.

The anchor for the present work is Chen et al., who examined consumer responses to
explainable AI recommendations across four studies \cite{chen2024}. The presence of post
hoc explanations increased interpretability perceptions and, through them, trust,
purchase intention, and click-through; the facilitating effect was stronger in the
utilitarian than in the hedonic decision-making domain; and explanation type modulated
effectiveness, with attribute-based explanations more effective at enhancing trust in
utilitarian domains and user-based explanations more effective in hedonic ones. Their own
conclusion is that there are no universally optimal post hoc explanations. The present
study carries this line from static recommendation displays into multi-turn conversation
and, as Section~\ref{sec:results} reports, obtains a result that runs against the
direction their third finding would predict.

\subsection{Explanations in Conversational Recommender Systems}

Jannach et al., surveying the CRS field, observe that although explanations are often
considered a main feature of a convincing dialogue, these aspects are not explored a great
deal \cite{jannach2021}. The intervening years have narrowed the gap only partially.

Hernandez-Bocanegra and Ziegler investigated conversational explanation delivery directly,
contrasting a GUI-based dialogue with a chatbot-like natural-language interface while
varying the degree of interactivity, and relating perceived explanation quality to
transparency, trust, and effectiveness through structural equation modelling
\cite{ziegler2023}. Providing interactive options for scrutinising explanatory arguments
had a significant positive influence on user evaluations. Their manipulation, however,
concerns interface and interactivity rather than the content of the explanation.

Siro et al. annotated CRS dialogues along six aspects --- relevance, interestingness,
understanding, task completion, interest arousal, and efficiency --- at both turn and
dialogue level, and found that the concept of satisfaction varies per user, with
relevance the significant factor at turn level \cite{siro2023}. Rana et al. studied
critiquing-based conversational recommendation with 228 participants and showed that the
choice of underlying recommendation algorithm materially affects critiquing behaviour,
failure rates, dialogue length, and satisfaction \cite{rana2024}.

Methodologically the closest parallel is Ziegfeld et al., who built three versions of a
CRS and ran a between-subjects experiment with 66 participants across ten user experience
constructs \cite{ziegfeld2025}. They found no significant effects of the three preference
elicitation methods on any questionnaire-based construct, although objective measures such
as chat duration and recommendation match score did differ. Their manipulation targeted
preference elicitation rather than justification content; the contrast with the present
findings is taken up in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\subsection{Trust and Purchase Intention in Recommender Systems}

Trust in a technological artefact is conventionally decomposed into beliefs about
competence, benevolence, and integrity \cite{mcknight2002}, and this structure underlies
the explanation-to-trust mapping reported in \cite{wang2007}. For user-centric evaluation
of recommenders, the ResQue framework supplies validated constructs together with design
guidance, including the guidance that explaining \textit{why} the system recommends an
item increases transparency whereas explaining \textit{trade-offs} increases persuasion
\cite{pu2012} --- a distinction that maps closely onto the comparison drawn in this study.
Perceived usefulness is taken in the technology acceptance sense, as the degree to which a
person believes that using a system would enhance performance \cite{davis1989}.

The presumed chain from trust to purchase behaviour has been validated in the chatbot
context by Yen and Chiang, who combined 204 questionnaire responses with EEG evidence and
found credibility, competence, anthropomorphism, social presence, and informativeness to
influence trust in chatbots, which in turn affected purchase intention \cite{yen2021}.
Notably, neither media richness nor playfulness positively influenced trust, which the
authors attribute to communication with a shopping chatbot being a functional task. This
is an early signal of the pattern the present study observes.

Work on anthropomorphism qualifies the chain further. Cheng et al. found perceived warmth
and perceived competence to positively affect trust in chatbots while communication delay
reduced it, and, critically, that exchange relationships strengthen the importance of
perceived competence on trust whereas communal relationships do not moderate the effect
of warmth \cite{cheng2022}. Li et al. modelled parallel pathways in which chatbot warmth
and competence affect both trust and chatbot overload, with both in turn shaping service
evaluation and then customer purchase, implying that trust is not the sole channel to
behavioural outcomes \cite{liyang2025}. Crolic et al. provide the strongest cautionary
precedent: across five studies, when customers enter a chatbot-led interaction in an angry
emotional state, chatbot anthropomorphism has a negative effect on satisfaction, firm
evaluation, and subsequent purchase intentions, driven by expectancy violations caused by
inflated pre-encounter expectations of chatbot efficacy \cite{crolic2022}. The premise
that a more human-seeming agent is a better agent is therefore already contested.

Most directly relevant, Li et al. examined anthropomorphic interaction style through the
lens of mind perception across three experiments \cite{libaoku2024}. For cognition-oriented
service tasks, a competent interaction style improved consumer responses more effectively
than a warm one, with agent-mind perception mediating; for emotion-oriented tasks a warm
style improved responses, mediated by experience-mind perception. Their claim that no
style is universally superior, and that task type sets the boundary, supplies the
mechanism this paper invokes in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\subsection{Sensory and Hedonic Product Domains}

Hedonic products are judged against criteria that are subjective and unstable relative to
the practical and comparatively stable criteria applied to utilitarian goods. Wang et al.
showed across three studies that perceived deception damages online repurchase intention
less for hedonic than for utilitarian products, attributing the difference to hedonic
evaluation being more fluctuant and susceptible to change \cite{wangx2022}. Using
clickstream data from a major online retailer, Varga and Albuquerque found that negative
reviews significantly reduce purchase probability and increase search for substitutes, but
that these effects apply to utilitarian products and not to hedonic ones, and to reviews
concerning product functionality or customer service but not to taste-related factors
\cite{varga2024}. Chen et al., as noted, found the effect of explanation itself to be
domain-contingent \cite{chen2024}.

The cumulative implication is that XAI evidence gathered in rational domains cannot be
assumed to transfer to sensory ones. To the authors' knowledge, no comparative test of
XAI explanation types has been reported in the olfactory domain.

\subsection{Familiarity as a Boundary Condition}

Domain familiarity is a plausible moderator of explanation effectiveness. Using an
AI-based decision support system in the chess domain with 100 participants, Bayer et al.
found that domain-specific expertise negatively affects trust, and, more pointedly for the
present design, that users with high domain-specific knowledge are more willing to trust
the system when an explanation is provided whereas users with low domain-specific
knowledge are not \cite{bayer2023}. Their conclusion is that explanations must fit the
user.

Celar and Byrne provide the closest design analogue, crossing explanation type with domain
familiarity across four experiments with 731 participants \cite{celar2023}. Participants
judged counterfactual explanations more helpful than causal ones; counterfactuals did not
improve the accuracy of their predictions of the AI's decisions, but did improve the
accuracy of their own decisions; and the effect of familiarity depended on whether the AI
was correct. Their sample size is a useful reference point when interpreting null
moderation results obtained on smaller samples.

\subsection{Research Positioning}

Three antecedents locate this study most precisely. Wang and Benbasat \cite{wang2007}
compared explanation types and decomposed trust into three dimensions, but did so on a
static recommendation agent in a utilitarian domain. Chen et al. \cite{chen2024} compared
domains and crossed presence with type, but on static recommendation displays rather than
in dialogue. Ziegfeld et al. \cite{ziegfeld2025} ran a between-subjects experiment inside a
multi-turn CRS, but manipulated preference elicitation rather than justification, and
found no effect on subjective measures.

The present study combines the three properties that these antecedents hold separately:
justification types are compared against one another rather than against a
no-explanation baseline; the comparison takes place inside a multi-turn, LLM-driven
conversation; and the domain is sensory-hedonic, with a core attribute that cannot be
conveyed through the interface at all. In addition, the narrative condition operationalises
affective explanation \textit{content}, which is distinct from the conversational
\textit{style} manipulations that dominate the anthropomorphism literature
\cite{libaoku2024}, \cite{cheng2022}.

\section{System Design}
\label{sec:system}

\subsection{Architecture Overview}

The experimental platform is Aromatique AI, a conversational recommender agent for
perfume deployed as a web application and operated on site in a physical perfume
store. Its architecture separates two responsibilities that are frequently fused in
LLM-based recommenders: \textit{language} and \textit{recommendation}. A large
language model, GPT-4o-mini, conducts the dialogue, extracts preferences, and
verbalises the justification. A separate backend, a conversational-recommendation
hybrid knowledge graph embedding (CR-HKGE) module, selects the recommended items.
Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} shows the arrangement.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig_architecture}}
\caption{System architecture. The LLM conducts the dialogue and phrases the
justification; item selection is delegated to the CR-HKGE backend and follows the same

\label{fig:arch}
\end{figure}

Two properties of this arrangement matter for the interpretation of the experiment.

First, \textit{the backend is a knowledge graph embedding model, not a
retrieval-augmented generation pipeline}. Recommendations are produced by scoring
candidate items in a learned embedding space over a structured perfume knowledge graph,
rather than by retrieving and summarising documents. The graph holds 998 entities and
9,255 triples across seven relation types. Its entities comprise 340 catalogue
products, 312 attribute entities on the catalogue side (17 olfactory families, 110
accords, and 185 visual notes), and a cross-reference layer of 346 entities (241
externally recognised perfumes with their 60 accords and 45 families) that supports
inspired-by and semantic-similarity links. Selecting three items from a catalogue of
340 is thus a non-trivial retrieval problem rather than a choice among a handful of
alternatives. The design follows the family of knowledge-graph-based recommenders that
aggregate collaborative and semantic signals through graph attention
\cite{litois2023}, \cite{elahi2024}, including work that extends such graphs to
conversational settings in order to capture implicit user states from dialogue context
\cite{yang2025}.

Second, \textit{the LLM acts as an interface rather than as the recommender}. This
mirrors the agent pattern in which an LLM serves as the reasoning layer and
domain-specific recommendation models serve as tools \cite{huang2023}, an arrangement
motivated by the observation that LLMs lack domain-specific item catalogues and are
prone to proposing items absent from the target catalogue. Item selection is therefore
governed by a single scoring procedure that is identical in all three conditions; the
LLM cannot substitute, reorder, or invent products.

It is important to be precise about what this does and does not guarantee. The
recommendation \textit{algorithm} is invariant across conditions. The recommended
\textit{items} are not guaranteed to be, because the backend is queried with a
preference representation that the dialogue itself produces, and the dialogue differs
between conditions in the manner described below. This qualification is treated
explicitly in Section~\ref{sec:discussion} rather than assumed away.

\subsection{Experimental Conditions}

The condition is set by system-prompt conditioning at the start of each session, with
no fine-tuning of the underlying model. This follows the prompting paradigm, one of the
three paradigms, alongside pre-training and fine-tuning, by which LLMs are adapted to
recommendation tasks \cite{zhao2024}. The practical advantage is that the independent
variable becomes inexpensive to manipulate and straightforward for other researchers to
replicate; the three prompts are reproduced in full in the appendix.

The manipulated variable is the \textit{justification logic} the agent applies, and it
is applied consistently across the whole interaction rather than only at the moment of
recommendation. Each prompt instructs the agent to maintain its assigned logic ``from
your first greeting to your final closing message.'' In practice this means the ten
elicitation questions are framed differently in each condition, as are the wording of
acknowledgements and the final explanation. Table~\ref{tab:conditions} illustrates this
with the parallel form of a single elicitation question and the corresponding
explanation style.

This is a deliberate design choice rather than an oversight. A system whose
conversational framing contradicted its own explanations would be internally
incoherent, and would be unlikely to occur in deployment. The cost of the choice is
that the study manipulates justification logic as a whole and therefore cannot separate
the contribution of explanation content from that of conversational framing. This
limitation is stated in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

Conditions A and C have established counterparts in the XAI literature. Condition A
corresponds to attribute-based or feature-importance explanation, the most extensively
studied form and the one shown to yield higher trust than counterfactual explanation
among non-expert users \cite{brito2023}. Condition C corresponds to contrastive
explanation, for which a two-level taxonomy of generation methods has been established
\cite{stepin2021}, and which has been shown to improve the accuracy of users' own
decisions relative to causal explanation \cite{celar2023}.

Condition B is the novel element. The CRS and anthropomorphism literatures examine
conversational \textit{style} along the warmth-competence axis \cite{libaoku2024},
\cite{cheng2022}, but treat explanation \textit{content} as fixed. Condition B instead
makes affective content the substance of the justification: it supplies no
compositional facts and no comparative frame, only resonance. It is the condition
closest in spirit to the user-based explanations found more effective in hedonic
domains \cite{chen2024}, while differing in one respect examined in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion}: user-based explanations convey social proof, whereas
purely narrative explanations convey none.

One asymmetry between the prompts must be recorded here because it bears on a
supplementary analysis reported later. The closing messages in conditions A and C
instruct the agent to end with an invitation to smell the fragrances at the store's
tester area; the closing message in condition B contains no such instruction. The
consequences of this asymmetry are examined in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\subsection{Conversational Interface}

A session proceeds in four stages: greeting and consent; preference elicitation;
presentation of three ranked perfumes together with the condition-appropriate
explanation; and the post-session questionnaire.

Elicitation follows a fixed protocol of exactly ten questions, asked one per message,
with the agent instructed never to bundle questions and to keep each reply to two or
three sentences. In the terms introduced by Ziegfeld et al. \cite{ziegfeld2025}, this
places the interface at the \textit{high-guidance, low-restriction} point of the
elicitation space: the sequence of topics is fixed by the system, while responses are
given in free-form natural language. The single exception is the first question, which
records perfume familiarity through three selectable tags corresponding to beginner,
enthusiast, and collector.

The dialogue yields a structured preference record comprising familiarity, occasion,
primary and secondary scent character, intensity, attributes to avoid, and free-text
additional context. Whereas the fields are identical across conditions, their content
is expressed in condition-specific vocabulary: scent character is captured as
attributes in condition A, as moods in condition B, and as positions on contrasting
spectra in condition C. This record is passed to the CR-HKGE backend, which returns
three ranked products with their olfactory family and main accords; the LLM then
phrases the justification according to its assigned logic.

Sessions comprised a mean of 20.29 messages exchanged between participant and agent
($SD = 4.67$, range 9 to 55). Dialogue length was comparable across conditions, with
means of 20.60 in condition A, 19.20 in condition B, and 21.07 in condition C, a spread
of under two messages. Interaction length is therefore an unlikely alternative
explanation for the differences reported in Section~\ref{sec:results}. Variability was
markedly greater in condition A ($SD = 7.03$) than in conditions B ($SD = 2.89$) and C
($SD = 2.65$), indicating a small number of unusually long feature-based sessions
rather than a systematic difference in dialogue length.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Experimental Design}

The study uses a single-factor between-subjects design with three levels of
justification logic (A: feature-based; B: narrative-based; C: comparative-based).
Assignment to condition was determined by a session counter, producing balanced
allocation of 30 participants per condition.

A between-subjects design was chosen over a within-subjects alternative deliberately.
Were a participant to experience all three modes, the comparison would become explicit
to them, inviting demand characteristics and carryover in how the later modes are
judged. The cost of this choice is reduced statistical power, particularly for the
interaction test reported in Section~\ref{sec:results}, and this cost is revisited in
the limitations.

\subsection{Participants}

Ninety participants completed the study, 30 in each condition. Perfume familiarity was
recorded at the first elicitation question using three selectable categories, and
recruitment was balanced by quota across them: 30 beginners, 30 enthusiasts, and 30
collectors.

For analysis these three levels were dichotomised, with beginners coded \textit{novice}
(n = 30) and enthusiasts and collectors together coded \textit{enthusiast} (n = 60).
The dichotomisation is justified on construct grounds rather than convenience. What
distinguishes the groups for present purposes is not intensity of interest but command
of olfactory vocabulary. Enthusiasts and collectors alike understand terms such as
accord, sillage, and top, heart, and base notes; beginners generally do not. Because
condition A rests entirely on that vocabulary, the boundary that matters falls between
beginners and everyone else.

Two consequences follow, and both are relevant to the interpretation of the moderation
test. First, the two-to-one imbalance between enthusiast and novice is a downstream
consequence of this coding decision, not a failure of recruitment; the sample itself was
balanced by design. Second, the resulting cells are small on the novice side, with 10,
12, and 8 novices in conditions A, B, and C respectively, against 20, 18, and 22
enthusiasts.

Familiarity was nonetheless distributed evenly across conditions,
$\chi^{2}(2) = 1.200$, $p = .549$, Cram\'{e}r's $V = 0.116$, with all expected counts at
or above 10. Randomisation therefore succeeded on this dimension, which supports the
interpretation that differences between conditions are not attributable to imbalance in
sample composition.

Demographic characteristics beyond familiarity, specifically age and gender, were not
recorded; the consequences are stated in the limitations.

Participation was voluntary and preceded by an on-screen consent notice stating that
the system formed part of collaborative academic research at the authors' institution,
that all responses would be fully anonymised, that the data would be used for academic
purposes only, and that participation could be declined. Participants proceeded only
after explicit affirmation. No personally identifying information was collected,
responses were anonymised at the point of capture, and no compensation was offered that
might have induced participation. The study involved no deception beyond withholding
the research hypothesis, no sensitive personal data, and no vulnerable population.
Formal institutional ethics review was not sought prior to data collection.

\subsection{Measures}

eight items in total. Trust items were adapted from the trusting-beliefs tradition
\cite{mcknight2002}; purchase intention and satisfaction items were adapted from the
ResQue framework for user-centric evaluation of recommender systems \cite{pu2012}; and
usefulness items were adapted from the technology acceptance tradition
\cite{davis1989}. Table~\ref{tab:items} lists the items.

One additional item, labelled \texttt{scent\_trial}, recorded whether the participant
had actually smelled the recommended product. It is important to be precise about what
this item measures: it is a record of \textit{sensory access already obtained}, not a
statement of intention to obtain it. In the present setting that distinction is
meaningful, because the study took place in a store where the fragrances were
physically available. The item therefore indicates whether the participant held
information the interface itself could not supply, and it is treated as exploratory
throughout.

\subsection{Procedure}

The study was conducted offline, on the premises of a physical perfume store, with the
system running as a web application on site. Data collection took place during the
store's normal operating sessions.

Each participant read the consent notice, affirmed participation, and then interacted
with the agent through the ten-question elicitation sequence until three ranked
recommendations and their accompanying explanations were delivered. The questionnaire
followed immediately. The consent screen advertised a duration of approximately ten
minutes. Because the store's tester area was accessible throughout, participants were
able to smell the recommended fragrances physically, and the \texttt{scent\_trial} item
records whether they had done so.

Participation arose from an institutional collaboration with the store rather than from
a recruitment campaign, and no compensation was offered. Participants were not told
that justification logic was under investigation, nor that other versions of the system
existed; the prompts explicitly forbid the agent from disclosing which style it is
using.

\subsection{Manipulation Check}

To verify that the three conditions were distinguishable from the transcripts alone, a
subsample of 30 session transcripts, 10 per condition, was coded independently by
three sources: the ground-truth condition assignment recorded by the system
(\textit{actual}), a coding by the researcher (\textit{self}), and a coding by an
independent rater blind to condition assignment (\textit{rater}). Agreement was
quantified with Cohen's kappa and interpreted against the conventional thresholds of
Landis and Koch \cite{landis1977}.

The researcher's dual role as both experimenter and coder is acknowledged explicitly.
Because self-coding cannot be considered blind, the evidentiary weight of the
manipulation check rests on the independent rater, and both the rater-based and the
self-based coefficients are reported separately in Section~\ref{sec:results} rather
than averaged into a single figure.

\subsection{Analysis Plan}

\subsubsection{Scale reliability}
Cronbach's alpha is not appropriate for two-item scales, where it is downward-biased
and unstable. Reliability was therefore assessed with the Spearman-Brown coefficient,
which Eisinga et al. identify as the appropriate estimator for two-item scales
\cite{eisinga2013}.
Average inter-item correlations are reported alongside the coefficients, since with
two items the coefficient is a direct function of a single correlation and the
correlation is the more interpretable quantity.

\subsubsection{Choice of non-parametric tests}
Shapiro-Wilk tests indicated departures from normality in several condition cells, and
Levene's test indicated heterogeneity of variance for perceived trust. Rank-based
procedures were therefore adopted for hypothesis testing: Kruskal-Wallis for
comparisons across the three conditions, Dunn's test with Holm correction for pairwise
comparisons, Spearman's rho for association, and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for
comparisons against a fixed reference value. Parametric analyses of variance were also
computed and are reported for reference, but inference rests on the rank-based results.

\subsubsection{Effect size}
For Kruskal-Wallis, the epsilon-squared statistic was computed as
$\varepsilon^{2} = H/(n-1)$, the rank-based analogue of eta-squared. Effect sizes are
reported for every omnibus test, since a p-value indicates only that an effect exists
whereas $\varepsilon^{2}$ indicates its magnitude. For pairwise comparisons, the
rank-biserial correlation is reported.

\subsubsection{Moderation}
The moderation hypothesis was tested with a two-way analysis of variance crossing
condition with dichotomised familiarity. This introduces an acknowledged inconsistency:
the main analyses are rank-based because normality was violated, whereas the moderation
test is parametric. The reason is practical rather than principled, in that no
comparably established rank-based test exists for a two-factor interaction. The
inconsistency is flagged here rather than left for the reader to discover, and the
result is interpreted with corresponding caution.

All analyses were conducted in JASP v0.97.1.
